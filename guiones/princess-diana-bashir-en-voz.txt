For twenty-nine years, the world has believed one story. Princess Diana died in a Paris tunnel on August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven, because her driver was drunk and going way too fast. Her two boys grew up with that story, and so did the rest of us.

That story is true. But it isn't all of it.

Two years before the crash, Diana sat down in a lawyer's office in London and described, in detail, how she expected to die. In a car. With the brakes tampered with. The lawyer wrote it down, signed it, dated it, and after she died he handed that sheet of paper to the chief of Scotland Yard, who locked it in a safe. Nobody outside that building saw it for eight years.

And the fear on that page didn't come out of nowhere. Somebody put it in her head. On purpose. With forged documents. And the man who did it was the one person she still trusted when everybody else was gone.

Stay with me until the end, because a retired judge finally wrote down, word for word, what that man told her, and when you put his words next to the lawyer's note, the two documents match.

To understand how a woman who had the world at her feet ended up in that car with no police officer anywhere near her, you have to go back to the night her body came home.

Sunday evening, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven. A Royal Air Force plane landed at Northolt, outside London, with Diana's coffin under the royal standard. From there, the hearse drove to the Hammersmith and Fulham mortuary. That night, a pathologist named Robert Chapman pulled on his gloves and examined the most photographed woman on earth, inch by inch.

What he found inside her answered one question. It left another one open. And the second one is the one this video is about.

The woman on that steel table in Fulham was born thirty-six years earlier, in a bedroom at Park House. It was a big stone house on the royal estate at Sandringham, in Norfolk, and on Saturday, July first, nineteen sixty-one, Diana Frances Spencer was born there.

Her father, John Spencer, and her mother, Frances, had badly wanted a boy. A year and a half earlier, they had buried a son, also named John, who lived for ten hours. When the midwife announced it was another girl, the house greeted Diana with more quiet than celebration.

She grew up feeling like she'd arrived in somebody else's place. And that feeling, of being one step outside of everything, would be the exact door the most dangerous man in her life would walk through.

In nineteen sixty-seven, when Diana was six, her mother left Park House. Diana said later that she sat on the bottom step of the staircase, listening to the tires on the gravel driveway getting farther and farther away. She waited on that step all afternoon.

That night, her little brother Charles, who was three, cried in the next room, calling for his mother. Diana heard all of it, lying in the dark, too scared to get up and cross the hallway. She carried the guilt of those nights for years.

In nineteen sixty-nine, her father won custody in court. Her mother could only see the children on certain weekends. The girl learned to listen at doors, to read the faces of adults, and to be wary of anyone who smiled too much.

That little boy crying for his mother in the next room, Charles Spencer, would grow up, inherit his father's title, and twenty-eight years later open the door of his own house to the man who would wreck his sister's peace.

In nineteen seventy-five, her father became an earl, and the family moved to Althorp, an enormous country house in Northamptonshire. Diana was fourteen, with a simple dream she repeated to her school friends. Marry for love and have a lot of kids.

Althorp is where everything started to go wrong for her. It's also the ground she'd be buried in, on an island in the middle of a lake.

The rest, the world knows. On Wednesday, July twenty-ninth, nineteen eighty-one, at twenty years old, Diana walked up the aisle of St. Paul's Cathedral in London in a dress with a train more than twenty-five feet long, in front of seven hundred fifty million people watching on television. At the altar, she was so nervous she got the groom's names in the wrong order. The groom was Prince Charles.

On Monday, June twenty-first, nineteen eighty-two, William was born, at a hospital in Paddington. Two years later, on Saturday, September fifteenth, nineteen eighty-four, Harry. Diana insisted on taking the boys to school herself, eating hamburgers with them, riding roller coasters, things no princess had ever done.

Those two boys, fifteen and twelve, were the ones she would call from Paris for the last time.

Inside, the marriage was already broken. Her husband was still in love with another woman, Camilla Parker Bowles, and Diana, alone in the palace hallways, was making herself throw up after meals. In December of nineteen ninety-two, the Prime Minister stood up in Parliament and announced that the couple was separating.

After that, her phone calls, secretly recorded, started showing up in the newspapers. Diana came to believe the phones at Kensington Palace were tapped, that the staff reported everything, that someone was following her car around the city. It was the old fear of the girl on the staircase, now with real reasons behind it.

And there was a man in London who knew exactly where that fear hurt. And he was willing to lie to make it grow.

His name was Martin Bashir. He was thirty-two, a reporter for Panorama, the most respected news program on the BBC, and he wanted one thing. The interview every journalist on earth wanted. Diana, on camera, talking about what she'd been through inside the royal family.

To get to the princess, Bashir went through the brother. On Thursday, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-five, he drove out to Althorp to meet Charles Spencer, who was thirty-one.

It was August thirty-first. Exactly two years, to the day, before Diana died.

The two men sat down in a room of the big house. Bashir opened an envelope and spread a stack of bank statements across the table. He explained, in a low voice, that the pages showed deposits into the account of Alan Waller, a former member of Charles Spencer's own security staff.

The money, according to Bashir, came from a company tied to a newspaper group, and from another firm nobody had heard of. The message was clear. Somebody was paying people on the inside to spy on the Spencer family. Charles Spencer looked at the numbers, the dates, the stamps, and he believed every line.

Those statements were fake.

Bashir had asked a graphic designer who worked for the BBC, Matt Wiessler, to mock up documents like that on a computer. Wiessler did what the reporter asked, thinking it was artwork for a story. The accounts, the deposits, the names on those pages, Bashir made up at an office desk to fool a brother who was scared for his sister. Twenty-six years later, in May of twenty twenty-one, a retired judge, Lord Dyson, put that in writing in an official report.

Charles Spencer would later say one sentence that he carries to this day like a stone on his chest.

If I hadn't seen those statements, I would never have introduced Martin Bashir to my sister.

But he did see them. And that same afternoon at Althorp, convinced, he promised to set up a meeting between the reporter and Diana in London. Bashir slid the papers back into the envelope, said thank you, and drove off down the same gravel road where, almost thirty years earlier, their mother's car had disappeared.

Hold on to those fake statements, because they're the start of the fear Diana carried into her lawyer's office two months later.

Nineteen days after that afternoon at Althorp, on Tuesday, September nineteenth, nineteen ninety-five, Diana crossed London to meet the man with the bank statements. The meeting was in a borrowed apartment in Knightsbridge, a few blocks from Harrods, far from the phones at Kensington Palace, which she no longer used for anything that mattered.

Charles Spencer was there, in an armchair by the window, with a notepad on his knee. He'd brought it to write down everything the reporter said, because he wanted to understand who this man was before he trusted him with his sister.

Those handwritten pages, scribbled in a hurry in that living room, would sit in a drawer for twenty-six years, until a judge read, line by line, what Martin Bashir told the princess that afternoon.

Diana arrived in dark glasses, sat on the couch and folded her hands in her lap. Bashir sat across from her, close, and started talking quietly, like a man sharing a family secret.

He told her that her phone was tapped. He told her that her car was followed around the city. And then he gave her names.

Patrick Jephson, Diana's private secretary, the man who had run her schedule for years, was supposedly being paid to pass along information about her. Richard Aylard, her husband's secretary, too. According to Bashir, both men were part of a network of people paid to watch the princess's every move.

Diana didn't argue. She looked at her brother for a while, and then down at her own hands. It was the girl on the staircase at Park House hearing, from a stranger, that a lifetime of fear had been right all along.

Charles Spencer left that apartment with a full notepad and a bad feeling in his stomach. The stories were too much, the names were too many, and he started to doubt the reporter who had won him over three weeks earlier with a stack of paper. In the days that followed, Charles Spencer pulled back.

Diana didn't.

Bashir started calling her. He met with her again, without the brother around. And inside Kensington Palace, Diana started looking at Patrick Jephson differently. Watching what she said in front of him. Sliding papers into a drawer when he walked into the room.

The man she now treated as a traitor was the very man who would be sitting next to her, six weeks later, when she said out loud how she thought she was going to die.

On Monday, October thirtieth, nineteen ninety-five, Diana walked into the office of Lord Victor Mishcon, in central London. Mishcon was eighty years old, one of the most respected lawyers in England, and he was handling her side of the separation. Jephson came in with her and took the chair next to hers.

The conversation started with the divorce, the paperwork, the boys. Then Diana leaned across the desk and changed the subject.

She said she'd received a warning from sources she considered reliable. The Queen was going to abdicate in April, and her husband was going to take the throne. And before then, someone was going to try to get rid of her.

Lord Mishcon picked up his pen. And Diana said the sentence he wrote down almost word for word, on a sheet of paper that would stay hidden for eight years.

An accident in my car, with the brakes prepared, or something like that.

She went on. If they couldn't kill her, they would at least leave her so injured, so shaken, that everyone would say the Princess of Wales had lost her mind.

Mishcon looked at Jephson. Jephson looked at Mishcon. Both men told her, as gently as they could, that it made no sense, that nobody was going to touch the brakes on the car of the future king's mother. Diana listened quietly, thanked them, and refused to say who had told her.

The lawyer wrote it all down, signed it, dated it, and put it away. Weeks after the tunnel, Mishcon handed that page to the police, who kept it in a safe until two thousand three.

Before that summer with Martin Bashir, Diana had never told anyone she was afraid of dying inside a car. Six weeks after the apartment in Knightsbridge, she was describing the brakes.

Twenty-two months after that conversation in Mishcon's office, the car hit the pillar.

Back to the mortuary in Fulham. Sunday night, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven. The royal coroner, John Burton, had the room opened. The man who put on the gloves was the pathologist Robert Chapman.

The body Chapman found on the steel table didn't look like the body of someone who had died in a crash that violent. On the outside, almost nothing. A few small marks, a bruise here, a scrape there. The face the whole world knew was practically untouched.

The damage was on the inside.

Her chest had already been opened in Paris. Chapman found the stitches that the surgeon Alain Pavie had put in during the night, in a small vein, about the width of a finger, at the exact spot where it enters the heart. It was the left superior pulmonary vein, the one that brings blood from the lungs back into the chest.

At the moment of impact, Diana's body was thrown forward so hard that her heart shifted inside her chest, and that vein tore. A small tear. But through it, blood leaked slowly into her chest cavity while she was still talking, still moving, still trapped in the wreck of the Mercedes.

The doctors in Paris had sewn that tear shut with their own hands. They massaged her heart, inside and out, for two hours. But by the time they reached the vein, the blood she'd lost wasn't coming back.

And there was one more thing about that body that Chapman noticed as soon as he began. A strong chemical smell that should not have been there.

Diana's body had been embalmed in Paris, hours before her family reached the hospital. The embalming fluid had spread through her tissues, and some of the tests Chapman wanted to run could no longer give a reliable result.

Because of that embalming, for years, Mohamed Al-Fayed repeated the same accusation to anyone who would listen. He was the father of Dodi, the boyfriend who died with Diana in the tunnel. He said Diana was pregnant with his son's child, and that the embalming had been rushed precisely to erase that.

But there was some of Diana's blood that no chemical had touched. On the night of the crash, while she bled, some of it ran down and pooled on the floor of the Mercedes, in the footwell, behind the front seats. It was blood from before any transfusion, before the hospital, before the embalming.

That blood was collected and tested. It had no pregnancy hormone in it.

So this is what Diana's body said on that table in Fulham. She died of a torn left pulmonary vein and internal bleeding, exactly as the Paris doctors had said. And she was not pregnant, because the blood left behind in the car, untouched, showed no sign of it. The embalming hid no baby. Chapman wrote the cause of death in one dry sentence.

A thirty-six-year-old woman with almost no marks on her face died because of a tear the size of a fingernail, while the world spent decades arguing about a pregnancy her own blood ruled out.

Two years before that steel table, Diana sat in front of an eighty-year-old lawyer and described, in detail, a car crash that was going to kill her, a few weeks after spending an afternoon listening to Martin Bashir tell her that everyone around her was a traitor. Who put that car in her head, and why, is written down in official documents.

Sunday, November fifth, nineteen ninety-five. Kensington Palace was almost empty. It was Bonfire Night, the English fireworks holiday, and Diana had given the staff the night off, one by one, until the only people left in apartments eight and nine were the ones she wanted there.

Late in the afternoon, a car pulled up to the gate. Martin Bashir got out with two other men, a producer and a cameraman, carrying boxes that, from a distance, looked like new stereo equipment being delivered. Inside were the camera, the lights and the microphones.

Patrick Jephson, the secretary who organized every minute of her life, was home that night. He wouldn't find out about those boxes for nine more days, when it was too late to do anything.

The three men set up in the first-floor sitting room. They drew the curtains, pushed the sofa into a corner, and set two chairs facing each other. Diana came downstairs in a dark suit, her eyes lined heavier than usual, and sat in the chair on the left.

She hadn't shown the questions to anyone. Lord Mishcon didn't know. Her press secretary didn't know. Her brother, who had backed away from Bashir after Knightsbridge, didn't know either. The only man in that room she trusted was the same one who, two months earlier, had spread fake bank statements across a table at Althorp.

Outside, over London, the first fireworks started going off. The cameraman pressed record.

Bashir started with the marriage. Then he asked about food, and Diana told him, looking straight at him, that in her first years at the palace she'd stuff herself and throw up four, five times a day. That she did it because she felt too small, too ugly, worthless.

He asked if she had ever hurt herself on purpose. Diana took a breath and answered without looking away.

I hurt my arms and my legs.

Then the conversation moved onto the ground Bashir had spent two months preparing. He asked whether there were people at the palace working against her. Diana said yes. She said the enemy was her husband's circle, that the people around her leaked everything, that her phone wasn't safe.

They were, almost word for word, the things she had heard in the apartment in Knightsbridge. Now they were on tape, in her voice, for the world to hear.

Bashir asked if she thought they would manage to push her aside. Diana smiled a little and said she wouldn't go quietly. She would fight to the end.

Then he asked whether Camilla Parker Bowles had been a factor in the breakdown of the marriage. Diana looked down, looked up again, and said the line that would travel around the world within hours.

Well, there were three of us in this marriage. So it was a bit crowded.

The recording went on for hours. By the time the three men took down the lights and carried the boxes back out to the car, the fireworks were over. Diana climbed the stairs to her bedroom alone, in a palace with no staff, certain she had done the right thing.

On Tuesday, November fourteenth, Diana called Patrick Jephson into her office. It was her husband's forty-seventh birthday. She told him, almost in passing, that she had given an interview to Panorama, and that it would air the following Monday.

Jephson stood there, frozen, with nothing to say. The man who had sat beside her in Mishcon's office, listening to her fear about the brakes, found out that morning that he'd been shut out of the most serious decision of her life. And he understood, from the way she looked at him, that she no longer trusted him.

He didn't know why. He still didn't know that Bashir, in a living room in Knightsbridge, had told the princess that her own secretary was being paid to spy on her.

Monday, November twentieth, nineteen ninety-five, was Queen Elizabeth and Prince Philip's forty-eighth wedding anniversary. The BBC schedule had Panorama at nine forty that night.

All over England, families ate dinner early. The pubs turned on the television. According to the next day's ratings, close to twenty-three million people watched, in the United Kingdom alone.

Diana watched at Kensington. Her sons were away. Harry, eleven, was at boarding school at Ludgrove. William, thirteen, was at Eton, in his first year there.

That night, William sat in his housemaster's study at Eton and watched his mother on the screen, eyes lined in black, telling millions of strangers about the vomiting, the cuts on her arms, and his father's mistress. He watched to the end without saying a word.

When Diana called afterward, the boy picked up, heard his mother's voice, and hung up.

Right after the program ended, Nicholas Soames, a government minister and a childhood friend of Prince Charles, went on live on Newsnight, also on the BBC. He said, on camera, that the princess was showing signs of the advanced stages of paranoia.

It was exactly what Diana had told Mishcon, three weeks earlier, that they would do to her. Call her crazy.

From that night on, every fear Bashir had watered seemed to come true. Her husband's friends attacked her on television. The next day's papers split the country down the middle. And inside the palace, the people who were still on her side started leaving.

Patrick Jephson wrote his resignation letter in December. He'd spent eight years at Diana's side, on the trips, in the hospitals, on the nights she cried in the car, and he walked out the door at Kensington without her asking him to stay. Her press secretary, Geoff Crawford, asked to leave too.

The week before Christmas, a messenger from Buckingham Palace crossed London with an envelope. Inside was a letter in the Queen's own handwriting. She sent identical copies to her son and her daughter-in-law, asking them both for a quick divorce, for the good of the country and the boys.

Diana read the letter sitting on her bed, alone.

Her brother was at Althorp, suspicious of the reporter he had introduced her to. Her secretary was gone. Her mother-in-law wanted a divorce. Her older son had hung up on her. In six weeks, the woman the world had watched on television was left with no one to trust inside her own home.

Only one phone kept ringing. It was Martin Bashir's.

Martin Bashir's phone kept ringing through nineteen ninety-six. Diana no longer used the landlines at Kensington for anything. She called him on her cell phone, almost always at night, sitting on the bedroom floor with her back against the bed, repeating what she'd been feeling. That someone was reading her mail. That someone was following her car.

On the other end, the reporter listened and agreed. Every time she hung up, she was more alone than before she'd called.

And every one of those late-night calls stripped one more layer of protection off her life, until all that was left, on the night in Paris, was one man in the front seat of a Mercedes.

On Wednesday, February twenty-eighth, nineteen ninety-six, Diana walked across the gardens to St. James's Palace to meet her husband. The aides waiting outside counted forty-five minutes behind a closed door. When she came out, she had her office tell the newspapers she was agreeing to the divorce, before Buckingham Palace was ready to say anything.

The Queen found out from the press. That same afternoon, Prince Charles's aides started describing the princess, off the record, as a woman out of control, unpredictable, who wouldn't listen to anyone.

It was the word she had predicted in Mishcon's office. Now she heard it from every direction, and she no longer had Patrick Jephson around to tell her she was overreacting.

The lawyers spent the spring fighting over numbers. By July, the deal was done. About seventeen million pounds, the apartment at Kensington, and the right to keep seeing her sons as she always had. In exchange, Diana lost the title of Her Royal Highness. She was still Princess of Wales in name. But to the Crown, she was no longer part of the family.

That same July, Diana wrote to more than a hundred charities to say she was stepping down as their patron. She kept six. The homeless, AIDS patients, cancer patients, leprosy patients, the Great Ormond Street children's hospital, and the English National Ballet.

On the morning of Wednesday, August twenty-eighth, nineteen ninety-six, at a family court office in Somerset House, on the banks of the Thames, a clerk stamped the final decree. Fifteen years of marriage ended with a stamp and a signature, with neither of them in the room.

Diana spent that morning in her office at Kensington. One of her secretaries said later that around noon, someone came in with boxes of new stationery. The crest was still there. The letters H.R.H. had vanished from the letterhead.

She picked up a sheet, looked at it for a while, and put it in a drawer without writing anything.

What hurt most that week came from a fourteen-year-old boy. William came into his mother's bedroom, sat on the edge of the bed, and said something Diana repeated to her friends until her last summer.

I don't care what they call you. To me, you're Mummy.

She cried in front of her son, something she had been avoiding for months.

There was one last thing standing between Diana and the world. The officers of Scotland Yard. Since nineteen ninety-three, she had been dismissing her bodyguards one at a time, convinced they reported to the palace where she went and who she talked to. After the divorce, the police offered to keep officers with her at official engagements. Diana said no.

One evening in September, an officer from royal protection came up to the office at Kensington with a form. He explained carefully what she was turning down. Diana signed it without reading to the end, handed back the pen, and thanked him. A year earlier, she had heard from Bashir that even her own secretary was being paid to watch her.

That signature cost her dearly. The men the police had trained to protect the future king's mother were on the other side of the English Channel the night the Mercedes entered the tunnel.

Diana started driving herself around the city. A friend of hers, Simone Simmons, said that that fall, the princess took her car to a garage outside London, far from the palace mechanics. She asked them to check the brakes, piece by piece. The mechanic found nothing. She paid in cash and asked him to check again.

It was the warning from Mishcon's office, now guiding her hands on the wheel.

In January of nineteen ninety-seven, Diana went to Angola with the Red Cross. On Wednesday, January fifteenth, in Huambo, she put on a blast vest and a plastic visor and walked slowly down a strip of red dirt cut through a minefield, in front of dozens of photographers.

There, with no officer of the Queen anywhere near her, she finally looked at peace. A minister in the Conservative government called the princess a loose cannon. She kept walking.

That spring, Diana was in love with a heart surgeon, a Pakistani named Hasnat Khan, who worked at the Royal Brompton Hospital. She talked about marrying him, about living with him far from England. But Khan couldn't stand the photographers, and by early summer, the two had drifted apart.

On Tuesday, July first, nineteen ninety-seven, Diana turned thirty-six. That night, she went to the Tate Gallery's hundredth-anniversary party in London in a short black dress, and came home early to Kensington. It was the last birthday she would have.

That same week, an invitation came from Mohamed Al-Fayed, the owner of Harrods, who had known the Spencer family for years. He offered his house in Saint-Tropez, in the south of France, for her to spend a few days with the boys. There she'd have something she no longer had in London. Security. Armed men. High gates.

Diana accepted. On Friday, July eleventh, she boarded Al-Fayed's private jet with William and Harry. On Monday, July fourteenth, his oldest son, Dodi, flew in from Paris.

Dodi was forty-two and a film producer. He was kind, laughed easily, and sent flowers every day. Diana spent her afternoons with him on the yacht Jonikal, while the boys dove off the deck.

Around them, the men now guarding the princess were Al-Fayed's team, on his payroll. One of them was a twenty-nine-year-old former soldier from Wales named Trevor Rees-Jones.

Six weeks later, Trevor Rees-Jones would be in the front passenger seat of the Mercedes, and he would be the only one to come out of the tunnel alive.

The summer went fast. On Tuesday, July twenty-second, Diana went to Milan for the funeral of the designer Gianni Versace, murdered on his own doorstep, and held Elton John as he wept in the cathedral pew. She went back to Dodi's yacht, then Paris, then London. In August, she spent three days in Bosnia, listening to widows and boys who had lost their legs to land mines.

In early August, an Italian photographer, hidden on a boat off Corsica, took the picture everybody knows. Diana and Dodi, embracing on deck, in swimsuits. The British papers paid a fortune for it. From that day on, every port the Jonikal pulled into woke up ringed with speedboats full of lenses.

Diana was happy and scared at the same time. She told a friend on the phone that she felt surrounded, that she couldn't go down to the beach anymore without seeing a boat following.

On Saturday, August thirtieth, nineteen ninety-seven, Al-Fayed's jet took off from Sardinia and landed that afternoon at Le Bourget, outside Paris. Diana came down the steps in dark glasses. At the bottom, the photographers' motorcycles were already revving.

She got into the back seat of a black car. Beside her, Dodi. In front, Trevor Rees-Jones. No officer from Scotland Yard, because she herself had signed that form at Kensington.

That evening, from the Ritz, she called her boys at Balmoral. William and Harry, fifteen and twelve. The call was short, because the boys were busy playing and wanted to get off the phone. They never heard their mother's voice again.

Diana went into Paris with the fear of the brakes still in her head. That fear wasn't only hers. Someone had put it there, for a reason, and that someone was the first to gain from every person she pushed away.

Ten o'clock on Saturday night, August thirtieth. Diana and Dodi got up from their table in the Ritz restaurant after ten minutes. People were staring. They went upstairs and had dinner in the suite. Out front, on the Place Vendôme, more than thirty photographers waited with their engines running.

Someone on the security team had the idea of leaving through the back. The camera on the rue Cambon door recorded the exit. Twenty minutes past midnight, Sunday, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven.

Diana crossed the sidewalk with her head down and got into the back seat of a black Mercedes. Dodi sat to her left. In front, Trevor Rees-Jones. At the wheel, Henri Paul, forty-one, the deputy head of security at the Ritz.

Neither of the two in the back seat buckled up.

The Mercedes went down through the Place de la Concorde and onto the expressway along the Seine. Behind it, the photographers' motorcycles were glued to the bumper. According to the French investigators, Henri Paul entered the tunnel under the Pont de l'Alma at more than sixty miles an hour, where the limit was thirty.

By the investigation's count, it was twelve twenty-three when the car lost control on the curve at the entrance. The Mercedes slammed head-on into the thirteenth concrete pillar of the tunnel, spun, and came to rest sideways, facing the way it had come.

For minutes, the horn blared without stopping. Henri Paul had fallen dead across the wheel.

Dodi was dead in the back seat. Trevor Rees-Jones, in front, had his face crushed against the dashboard, but he was breathing. And Diana was on the floor of the car, almost on her knees, in the gap between the seats, head down, hair across her face.

She was alive. Blood ran slowly onto the carpet, in the footwell. And it was that blood, collected right there, that would show years later that she wasn't pregnant.

The flashes started before the ambulance. A doctor who happened to be driving through the tunnel, Frédéric Mailliez, stopped, held her head, and called for help on his cell phone. Soon after came a fire department sergeant, Xavier Gourmelon, who knelt by the door and took Diana's hand.

She looked at him and said one sentence, in English, which Gourmelon repeated to a London jury eleven years later.

My God. What's happened?

When the firefighters got her out of the wreck and onto the stretcher, her heart stopped. Gourmelon pressed down on her chest with both hands, counting out loud, until it started beating again. He thought she was going to make it.

The ambulance left the tunnel more than an hour after the crash. It drove slowly, on the doctors' orders, because any jolt could kill her. Near the Jardin des Plantes, Diana's blood pressure dropped so far that the driver stopped in the middle of the street and the doctors worked with the vehicle standing still.

The emergency log read two oh six in the morning when the stretcher came through the doors of the Pitié-Salpêtrière hospital.

According to the surgeon Monsef Dahman, who was on duty, at two fifteen Diana's heart stopped again. There was no time to get her to an operating room. Dahman opened her chest right there, on the emergency room gurney.

The surgeon Alain Pavie came running from home. He spread the ribs, felt with his fingers through the blood, and found the tear in the left superior pulmonary vein, right where it meets the heart. He sewed it shut.

They shocked her. They gave her adrenaline. They squeezed her heart with their own hands, inside and out, for two hours.

At four in the morning, the doctors stopped. Diana was thirty-six. At six, the anesthesiologist Bruno Riou walked into a room full of reporters and announced the death of the Princess of Wales.

At Balmoral Castle, in Scotland, their father woke the boys in the morning. Harry, twelve, wrote in his memoir that his father sat on the edge of his bed and said, Darling boy, Mummy's been in a car crash. And then, They tried, darling boy. I'm afraid she didn't make it.

Harry didn't cry. It would be years before he could cry for his mother.

In Paris, Sunday dawned hot. Diana was moved to a room on the first floor of the hospital, with windows facing the sun. The nurses said later that by early afternoon the room was past eighty-five degrees, and that they put dry ice under the sheet.

No Spencer had reached Paris yet. Her ex-husband was still in Scotland with the boys.

It was in those hours that an embalmer from a French funeral company, Jean Monceau, walked into the room with a metal case. The door stayed closed for close to two hours. When he came out, Diana's blood had been replaced with a chemical fluid, and her body was no longer the one that had come off the gurney.

French law requires the family's authorization to embalm someone. The family wasn't there. The official investigation said the decision was made because of the heat, ahead of the relatives' visit.

The woman who had spent two years afraid of people acting behind her back was lying in a stifling room while a man she'd never met worked on her body.

Around five in the afternoon, Prince Charles walked into the hospital with Diana's two sisters, Sarah and Jane. By the time they reached the bed, the work of the metal case was already done.

That same night, the body was in Fulham, and the smell of the fluid was the first thing Chapman wrote down.

In London, the coffin lay in the Chapel Royal at St. James's Palace. Outside Kensington Palace, people left flowers until they covered the lawn, and the smell of the flowers reached the windows of the apartment where she had signed the police form.

On Saturday, September sixth, nineteen ninety-seven, the coffin left on a gun carriage pulled by horses. On top, white roses and an envelope with one word on it, handwritten by Harry. Mummy.

Behind the coffin, on foot, walked five men. Her ex-husband, his father Philip, her brother Charles Spencer, and the two boys, William and Harry, fifteen and twelve, in dark suits, eyes on the ground.

In Westminster Abbey, Charles Spencer stepped up to the pulpit. This was the brother who, two years earlier, had believed the fake statements at Althorp. He said his sister, who carried the name of the ancient goddess of hunting, had ended up the most hunted person of the modern age.

Outside, the crowd listening over the loudspeakers began to applaud, and the applause rolled in through the doors of the abbey until it reached the pews.

Afterward, the hearse drove up the highway to Althorp. Diana was buried on an island in the middle of the lake, on the ground where she'd spent her teenage years dreaming of marrying for love.

Twelve days later, on Thursday, September eighteenth, Lord Victor Mishcon, eighty-two years old, walked into Scotland Yard headquarters with a folder under his arm. He sat down across from the head of London's police, Sir Paul Condon, opened the folder, and handed over the page dated October thirtieth, nineteen ninety-five.

Condon read the princess's words about the brakes on her car and had the page locked in a safe.

That note held Diana's fear, word for word. The name of the man who had fed that fear was somewhere else. In a folder inside the BBC itself, with the Althorp statements tucked inside.

On Monday, April seventh, two thousand eight, eleven jurors filed into courtroom seventy-three of the High Court in London. They had spent six months hearing more than two hundred fifty witnesses. In the front row of the public gallery, in a dark suit, sat Mohamed Al-Fayed, chin resting on his hands.

The foreman stood and read the verdict in front of the judge, Lord Justice Scott Baker. Unlawful killing. Diana and Dodi had died through the grossly negligent driving of Henri Paul and of the vehicles chasing the Mercedes. And the absence of seat belts in the back seat had contributed to both deaths.

Nine jurors voted for it, two against.

The jury decided how Diana died. What they didn't hear was who had spent two years before the tunnel filling her head with fear. That part of the story was still sitting in a drawer.

Twelve years later, Matt Wiessler, the graphic designer who had mocked up the fake statements on his computer, decided to talk. He'd been pushed out of the BBC in nineteen ninety-six, and had spent nearly twenty-five years being treated as the guilty man in a story that wasn't his.

In November of twenty twenty, Charles Spencer opened a drawer at Althorp and took out the notepad from that afternoon in Knightsbridge. The pages were yellowed, his handwriting hurried, slanting to the right. He handed everything over to the BBC, and the BBC brought in a retired Supreme Court judge, Lord Dyson, to investigate.

On Thursday, May twentieth, twenty twenty-one, the Dyson Report was published. More than a hundred pages. And inside it, copied line by line, was what Martin Bashir told the princess on the couch of that borrowed apartment, on Tuesday, September nineteenth, nineteen ninety-five.

Her phone was tapped. Her car was followed. Patrick Jephson and Richard Aylard were being paid to watch her. And in the middle of the page, underlined by Charles Spencer's pen, there was one more thing the reporter said that afternoon.

The Queen had a heart condition. She was going to step down. Diana's husband was going to be king.

Six weeks later, in Lord Mishcon's office, Diana leaned across the desk and repeated that exact story. The Queen was going to abdicate in April. Her husband was going to take over. And before then, someone was going to prepare the brakes on her car.

It was the same story, on both pieces of paper. The voice was Diana's. But the story had come out of Martin Bashir's mouth.

The Queen did not have a heart condition. Jephson never took a penny from anyone to spy on the princess. The Althorp statements were a forgery. Lord Dyson wrote that Bashir acted deceitfully, that he faked documents to get to Diana, and that the BBC itself, in an internal investigation in nineteen ninety-six, covered it all up and let the reporter carry on.

He did it to get an interview. To have, in a sitting room at Kensington, on a night of fireworks, the most famous woman in the world sitting in the chair on the left, afraid of everyone around her except him.

Diana got into that car in Paris carrying a fear that a journalist had manufactured for ratings.

That same night in May, William sat down in front of a camera, in a blue suit, hands clasped, and read a statement straight into the lens. This was the boy from Eton who had hung up on his mother, now thirty-eight, with three children of his own.

He said the reporter had made lurid and false claims about the royal family, which played on her fears and fueled paranoia. And he said it brought him indescribable sadness to know that the BBC's failures contributed significantly to the fear, paranoia and isolation he remembered from those final years with his mother.

Then William paused, and said the line that weighed the most.

If the BBC had properly investigated the complaints raised in nineteen ninety-five, my mother would have known that she had been deceived.

She died without knowing. She died believing Patrick Jephson had betrayed her, that the officers of Scotland Yard reported her movements to the palace, that someone was going to tamper with the brakes. She died without knowing that the one man she trusted that fall was the one man lying to her.

The BBC promised never to air that interview again. Martin Bashir left the network a few days before the report came out. And in a Scotland Yard file, inside a folder, the page Lord Mishcon dated Monday, October thirtieth, nineteen ninety-five, is still there.

The old lawyer's handwriting is still on it, a little faded. An accident in my car, with the brakes prepared, or something like that.

The girl who waited on the bottom step for a mother who didn't come back became the woman who sent away, one by one, every person who was actually protecting her.

Diana spent two years afraid of the wrong car. The real danger was a man she let in because he said he was looking out for her. The fear she carried into that lawyer's office is the fear that emptied the front seat of the Mercedes of anyone trained to keep her alive.
