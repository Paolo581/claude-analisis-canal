# Freddie Mercury Took One Secret to His Grave. 34 Years Later, His Notebooks Revealed It.

English narration script, retention-edited from the Portuguese original. Numbers spelled out for the voice. Paragraph breaks mark the pauses. Claims that come from the 2025 book are attributed to it on purpose; see the note at the end before cutting.

## INTRO

On the evening of November twenty-fourth, nineteen ninety-one, in a bedroom in West London, the greatest voice in the history of rock went quiet. Freddie Mercury was forty-five years old. He had told the world he was dying only the day before.

But there was one thing he never told the world at all.

Not the band he had played with for twenty years. Not the press that had hunted him for a decade. Not even, until the very end, the two people sitting beside his bed.

He wrote it down instead. Seventeen notebooks, filled by hand, kept hidden until the last day of his life.

And in September of twenty twenty-five, thirty-four years after his death, a book published in England finally opened them.

Stay with me until the end, because what was inside those notebooks changes the story you think you know about Freddie Mercury. And it starts with a date. Nineteen seventy-six. The year he confessed one secret to the woman he loved, and the year, according to those notebooks, he started keeping a much bigger one.

But to understand why a man who sang to two billion people hid this until he died, we have to go back to the beginning. To a boy with a different name, on the other side of the world.

His name was Farrokh Bulsara. He was born on September fifth, nineteen forty-six, on the island of Zanzibar, just off the coast of East Africa, in what is now Tanzania.

His parents were Parsis, Indians of the Zoroastrian faith, an ancient people who had fled Persia centuries earlier to escape religious persecution. His father worked as a clerk for the British colonial government.

At eight years old, Farrokh was sent away. To a boarding school in Panchgani, India, more than twenty-five hundred miles from his parents. He stayed there nearly eight years, seeing his mother and father only on holidays.

Hold on to that image. An eight-year-old boy, put on a boat, sent to grow up among strangers. Because the man he became would spend his whole life building families out of strangers, and then hiding the most important ones.

At that school he learned piano, formed his first band, and picked up the name the other boys gave him. Freddie. He also learned to live with the thing he was most ashamed of. He had been born with four extra teeth at the back of his mouth, which pushed his front teeth forward. The boys called him Bucky. He would cover his mouth with his hand for the rest of his life. But he refused, even when he was rich, to have them fixed. He was convinced those teeth gave him his range.

In nineteen sixty-four, a violent revolution tore through Zanzibar. Thousands were killed in a matter of days. The family of a British government clerk had every reason to run. The Bulsaras fled with what they could carry and landed in London.

And it was in London that Farrokh Bulsara found the two things that would define his life. A band that would become Queen. And a woman he would call, until the day he died, the love of his life.

## PART 1

Nineteen sixty-four. A seventeen-year-old Parsi boy steps off a plane in London. He's skinny, with an overbite so bad he covers his mouth when he laughs. Nobody gives him a second look.

Inside, he's carrying a dream so big he's embarrassed to say it out loud.

Two years later he enrolls at Ealing Art College. And in those hallways, he starts erasing himself. The name Farrokh, which nobody in England can pronounce, quietly goes away. He's Freddie now. Freddie Bulsara.

In nineteen sixty-nine, Freddie walks into a boutique on Kensington High Street called Biba. It's the trendiest store in London. Behind the counter is a nineteen-year-old named Mary Austin.

He comes back the next day. And the day after that. He waits outside for her shift to end. He takes her to cheap little restaurants in Kensington. And slowly, the girl behind the counter and the skinny art student become a couple.

By day, Freddie and Roger Taylor run a vintage clothing stall at Kensington Market, selling old fur coats and velvet jackets to make rent. By night, Freddie is home, writing.

They move into a tiny apartment together. And here's the part almost nobody knows. For years, Mary Austin paid for everything. She worked the sales floor at Biba while Freddie sat at the kitchen table writing songs nobody had heard. The rent, the groceries, the beginning of the biggest band in the world. A nineteen-year-old retail clerk paid for it.

Around the same time, Freddie starts following a small London band called Smile. Two college students, Brian May on guitar and Roger Taylor on drums. Freddie goes to every show. And he's not shy about telling them what they're doing wrong.

In nineteen seventy, Smile's singer quits. Freddie makes his move. He tells Brian and Roger they should start a new band, with him out front. They say yes on the spot.

Then Freddie picks a name that makes everyone in the London rock scene cringe.

Queen.

In nineteen seventy, in a scene full of long-haired tough guys, it sounded ridiculous. Freddie wouldn't budge. He never really explained why. Keep that in mind, because the real reason is going to come out of Mary Austin's mouth six years later.

In nineteen seventy-one, a quiet electronics student named John Deacon tries out on bass. Freddie hires him on the spot. The lineup is set.

And that same year, Freddie does something that breaks his parents' hearts.

He drops the name Bulsara. Legally. Publicly. For good. He takes the name of a planet instead. Mercury.

For Bomi and Jer Bulsara, the family name carried two and a half thousand years of Parsi history. Their son had thrown it away to be a rock singer in a band called Queen. It was a wound in that household that never closed.

And while Queen started to rise, Mary Austin started to notice things.

Freddie was coming home later and later. He dodged every serious conversation about their future. He disappeared on weekends with new friends she never got to meet.

What she didn't know yet was that her boyfriend already had a second life. In bars she would never set foot in. With men he introduced to her as just friends.

The night she finally asked him about it is the night this whole story turns.

## PART 2

Queen's first album came out in nineteen seventy-three and went nowhere.

Then, in nineteen seventy-four, came Killer Queen. A weird, theatrical song that had no business being a hit, and became the band's first hit around the world.

And in nineteen seventy-five, Freddie Mercury sat down and wrote the song that would outlive everyone in this story.

Bohemian Rhapsody. Six minutes long. Rock, ballad, opera, and back again. The label said radio would never touch it. It spent nine weeks at number one in the UK. Overnight, Queen was the biggest band on earth.

And the bigger Freddie got, the further he drifted from the woman who had paid for it all.

It hadn't always been that way. Two Christmases earlier, in nineteen seventy-three, Freddie had handed Mary a large box. Inside it was a smaller box. Inside that, another. At the bottom, a ring. He asked her to marry him. She said yes.

The wedding never happened. Freddie kept finding reasons to postpone it. Mary kept the ring.

The men around him kept multiplying. Mary knew. She'd known for a while. What she wanted was to hear it from him.

So one night in nineteen seventy-six, in the Kensington apartment, after seven years together, she sat Freddie Mercury down and asked him point-blank. Who are these men? And am I still the love of your life?

Freddie took a breath. And he told her he thought he was bisexual.

Mary Austin's answer is one of the bravest things anyone ever said to him.

She looked at him and said: No, Freddie. I don't think you're bisexual. I think you're gay.

That was the end of the romance. And the beginning of something stranger, and stronger, than most marriages.

They never fought about it. They spoke every day for the rest of his life. Freddie told her he would love her until he died, and he meant it literally.

Years later he explained it to a journalist in a way that nobody who heard it forgot. He said all of his lovers had asked him why they couldn't replace Mary, and that it was simply impossible. He said she was the only real friend he had. To him, he said, it was a marriage.

Mary put it even more simply. When he died, she said, she lost her family.

In nineteen eighty he bought a mansion in Kensington called Garden Lodge. And he bought Mary an apartment a few minutes' walk away, so she'd always be close. He had already written her a song, Love of My Life. He sang it at every Queen concert for the next fifteen years, and he was singing it to her.

If this story is already getting to you, hit the like button and subscribe. Because the year of that confession, nineteen seventy-six, is about to come back. And when it does, it's going to break your heart.

Through the early eighties, Queen became the biggest live act in the world. Stadiums on every continent. A voice nobody could touch.

And on July thirteenth, nineteen eighty-five, at Wembley Stadium, Freddie Mercury gave the greatest live performance in the history of rock.

Live Aid. Seventy-two thousand people in the stadium. Nearly two billion watching on television. Queen was given twenty minutes in the afternoon. By the end of those twenty minutes, Freddie Mercury owned the planet.

Here's what those two billion people didn't know. That morning, Freddie had a throat infection. His doctor told him not to sing. He sang anyway. Six songs in twenty-one minutes, and right in the middle, the call and response with the crowd, the "ay-oh," that is still the most famous sound ever made in a stadium.

Backstage afterward, Elton John walked into Queen's trailer and told them they'd stolen the show. Bob Geldof, who put the whole day together, said the same thing on the record.

What none of them knew was that, in a London hotel barbershop, Freddie had just met a quiet Irish hairdresser named Jim Hutton. Jim would move into Garden Lodge. He'd be there, in the bedroom, on the last day.

And what Freddie himself didn't know yet, as he strutted across that Wembley stage, was that his body had already started sending him signals.

Small things at first. Things he didn't tell anyone in the band.

Two years later, a doctor in London would put a name to them.

## PART 3

April, nineteen eighty-seven. Freddie Mercury is forty years old. He walks into a clinic in London for test results.

HIV positive.

In nineteen eighty-seven, that wasn't a diagnosis. It was a countdown. There was no treatment. The disease had already killed friends of his. The doctors told him he had a few years, and nobody could say how many.

What Freddie did next tells you everything about him.

He told Jim Hutton. He told Mary Austin. And he made them both swear it would never reach a journalist. He didn't tell Brian, Roger, or John. Not yet. He went back to work.

When he told Jim, he added one sentence. If you want to leave, I'll understand. Jim stayed. He would later find out he was HIV positive himself.

That same year, he met the Spanish soprano Montserrat Caballé, one of the greatest opera voices alive. Freddie, who'd been sneaking opera into rock music since Bohemian Rhapsody, was starstruck. He asked her to make an album with him.

The result was Barcelona. Released in nineteen eighty-eight. A rock singer and an opera diva, trading verses. The city of Barcelona adopted it for the nineteen ninety-two Olympic Games.

Here's the detail that hurts. Freddie knew, as he recorded it, that he wouldn't live to see those Games. He was writing a song for a celebration he would watch from nowhere. And in nineteen ninety-two, his voice did ring out over Barcelona, to a billion people, nine months after his funeral.

Meanwhile, the British tabloids had caught the scent.

The Sun started running stories about his health. Photographers camped outside Garden Lodge. Freddie's answer was to disappear. No interviews. No TV. He went behind the walls of his house and stayed there.

But the few photographs that got out told the story anyway. The face getting thinner. The cheekbones sharpening. Dark lesions on his skin that fans started to notice and argue about.

In early nineteen ninety-one, Queen released Innuendo. Freddie could barely stand in the studio. He insisted on recording every song.

One of them, Brian May had written for him. The Show Must Go On.

The day Freddie came in to record it, Brian was afraid to even ask him to try. The man in front of him had lost most of his body weight. Freddie listened to the track, said "I'll do it, darling," downed a shot of vodka, and nailed it in one take.

Listen to that recording again after this video. That is a dying man refusing to sound like one.

That spring, the band filmed a video for These Are the Days of Our Lives. It was shot in black and white, because color would have shown what the disease had done to his face. It is the last footage of Freddie Mercury ever filmed. In the final frame, he looks straight into the camera and whispers, "I still love you."

Then he went to Montreux, Switzerland, where Queen had a studio on the lake, and he gave the band one instruction. Write me anything. I'll sing it. Then I'll die. They recorded as much as he could stand. On a song called Mother Love, he sang the verses, told them he would come back the next day to finish the last one, and never came back. Brian May sang the final verse himself. It is the last thing Freddie Mercury ever recorded.

Brian, Roger, and John knew by then. Freddie had told them, in his own way, when he could no longer hide it in the studio. He asked them for one thing. Keep working. Don't make it a tragedy. Don't tell anyone.

On November twenty-third, nineteen ninety-one, he broke his own rule.

A statement went out to the press from Garden Lodge. Freddie Mercury confirmed he had AIDS. He asked for privacy for the people around him.

The world had less than twenty-four hours to absorb it.

## PART 4

November twenty-fourth, nineteen ninety-one. A cold Sunday in London.

In the main bedroom at Garden Lodge, Freddie is too weak to speak. Jim Hutton is there. Mary Austin has been coming every day. His closest friends take turns at the bedside.

Two weeks earlier, he had made a decision. He stopped taking every medication except the painkillers. He had seen what the drugs could and couldn't do, and he had decided to let it end on his own terms. He asked for his cats to be brought to the bed. He asked to be propped up so he could look at his paintings one more time.

At twelve minutes to seven in the evening, Freddie Mercury dies. The cause is bronchial pneumonia, brought on by AIDS. He is forty-five.

By midnight, radio stations around the world have thrown out their playlists to play Queen. By morning, it's the top story on every newscast in Britain.

And then his family discovers what he wanted done with his body.

Freddie had asked to be cremated.

For his parents, that was unthinkable. In the Zoroastrian tradition, the dead are not burned and not buried. They are laid out in Towers of Silence, open to the sky, and returned to nature by the birds. Fire is sacred. You do not give a body to it.

Freddie knew exactly what he was asking of them. He asked anyway.

And he asked one more thing. He told Mary Austin to take his ashes and hide them. To tell no one where. Not his family. Not the band. Not even Jim.

On November twenty-seventh, at Kensal Green in West London, a Zoroastrian priest chanted over his coffin in Avestan, a language none of the rock stars in the pews understood. Elton John was there. Brian, Roger, and John were there. Mary wept beside the coffin. The ceremony took twenty-five minutes. Then the body went to the crematorium.

Two pieces of music were played. A gospel hymn by Aretha Franklin, and an aria by Verdi, sung by Montserrat Caballé. Rock and opera, side by side, one last time.

Mary kept her promise. More than three decades later, nobody knows where Freddie Mercury's ashes are. Some say under a tree in the backyard at Garden Lodge. Some say Lake Geneva, near Montreux, where he had a home. Mary has never said a word.

She did say one thing, in twenty thirteen. She said she kept the urn in his bedroom for two years, because she couldn't bring herself to do it. Then one morning, without telling anyone in the house, she took it and went out. Nobody knows where she went.

That same year, something strange turned up at Kensal Green. A small plaque, hidden among the memorials. It read: In loving memory of Farrokh Bulsara. September fifth, nineteen forty-six, to November twenty-fourth, nineteen ninety-one. And then a line in French. To be always close to you, with all my love. Signed with a single letter. M.

Within days of the photographs appearing online, the plaque was gone.

Keep that letter M in mind. And keep the French. Because when the notebooks are opened, both of them are going to make you wonder.

Then they read the will.

Mary Austin got Garden Lodge. She got half of his future income, including his share of Queen's royalties. His parents and his sister Kashmira got the rest, split between them. Jim Hutton got five hundred thousand pounds and the right to stay in the house. For a while.

The girl from behind the counter at Biba, who had paid Freddie's rent in nineteen seventy, was now the keeper of his house, his ashes, and his fortune.

A month after he died, Queen re-released Bohemian Rhapsody, with every penny going to AIDS charities. It went right back to number one.

And on April twentieth, nineteen ninety-two, the three surviving members walked back onto the Wembley stage, where he had conquered the world seven years earlier, for a tribute concert. Seventy-two thousand people again. A billion watching again. David Bowie, Elton John, George Michael, Liza Minnelli, all of them singing his songs, and none of them able to sing them the way he had.

And somewhere inside Garden Lodge, according to the book that would come out thirty-four years later, there was a locked drawer nobody had opened yet.

## PART 5

Here's where the story leaves the official record, and I want to be straight with you about that.

Everything from this point comes from one source. A book called Love, Freddie, by the British journalist Lesley-Ann Jones, who has written about Mercury for decades. It was published in September of twenty twenty-five, around what would have been Freddie's seventy-ninth birthday. Neither Queen nor the Mercury estate has confirmed what it says. Several people who knew him have pushed back on it. You can judge for yourself.

According to the book, Freddie Mercury had a daughter.

She was born in nineteen seventy-six.

The same year Mary Austin sat him down and he came clean to her. The same year the romance ended.

Her mother, the book says, was a married woman. And her husband was one of Freddie's closest friends. The child came from an affair Freddie never admitted to anyone, and she was raised in Europe, far from the London press, under a name the book keeps secret. In the book, she is simply called B.

Freddie, the book says, visited her through the eighties. He called her his treasure. And through those years, he wrote to her. Not letters. Journals. Seventeen handwritten volumes, addressed to the daughter he couldn't acknowledge, covering the things he could never say in an interview. His childhood in Zanzibar. His fears. His illness. Why he hid.

The book says a handful of people in his inner circle knew. It doesn't say Mary Austin was one of them. It doesn't say she wasn't.

The seventeen notebooks from the start of this video.

According to the book, they were left to B. And B kept them, in silence, for more than thirty years, while she built a life as a medical professional in Europe, with two children of her own who didn't know whose grandchildren they were.

Then, in her late forties, she approached Lesley-Ann Jones. She handed over the notebooks. And she said she wanted the truth told while she could still tell it.

In the book, B describes a father who was present in ways the world never saw. Who turned up. Who wrote. Who, in her words, cherished and adored her. She says she kept quiet for thirty years because that's what he asked.

Now, if this is real, go back and listen to that last Queen album again. Innuendo, nineteen ninety-one. A dying man's record. There is a song on it called Bijou. The French word for a small jewel. And there is another called Don't Try So Hard, a man telling someone young not to carry the weight he carried.

The book has a theory about who those songs were for. Freddie never said.

And this is where I have to give you the hard part.

According to the reporting that followed the book, B didn't get to see what happened next. She fell ill with a rare cancer of the spine. In January of twenty twenty-six, four months after the book came out, she died. She was forty-eight. She left two young children. And according to those reports, her ashes were scattered in the mountains she loved.

She died without ever publishing a DNA test.

And that's why this story is still open.

## PART 6

After B died, the questions got louder.

People who'd been in the room at Garden Lodge in nineteen ninety-one said they'd never heard of a daughter. Brian May, when asked, said he knew nothing about it and found it hard to believe. Mary Austin, who has spent thirty-four years saying almost nothing to anyone, said nothing to this either.

So you're left with two possibilities, and both of them are extraordinary.

Either a woman invented a father, seventeen notebooks, and a childhood of secret visits, and took it to her grave.

Or Freddie Mercury, the most watched man in rock and roll, kept a child hidden from his band, his biographers, and the woman he called the love of his life, for fifteen years, and left the only proof in a drawer.

Here's what nobody disputes.

Queen has sold more than three hundred million records. Bohemian Rhapsody became the first song from the twentieth century to pass a billion views on YouTube. In twenty eighteen, a movie with the same name made more than nine hundred million dollars, and Rami Malek won the Oscar for playing him.

Mary Austin still lives at Garden Lodge. She never sold it. She has two sons of her own, born in the early nineties, raised in Freddie's house. His royalties have reportedly made her one of the wealthiest women in British music. And she still hasn't said where the ashes are.

And nobody has ever explained the plaque at Kensal Green. The French words. The single letter M. Mary has never claimed it. Whoever put it there, and whoever took it away, has never spoken.

Jim Hutton stayed in the house a few more years, then went home to Ireland. He died in twenty ten.

Freddie's parents are gone. His sister Kashmira is the last Bulsara.

Now go back to the beginning. To an eight-year-old boy on a boat to India, sent away from his mother and father to grow up among strangers.

That boy spent the rest of his life changing his name, hiding his accent, hiding his lovers, hiding his illness, hiding his own ashes. And if the notebooks are real, hiding a daughter.

Not one of those secrets was about shame. Every single one was about protection. He protected his parents from a name that embarrassed them. He protected Mary from a truth that would have humiliated her. He protected the band from a tragedy that would have ended them early. He protected the world from watching him die.

And if the book is right, he protected a little girl from the only thing he couldn't survive himself. Cameras.

The man who could hold seventy-two thousand people in the palm of his hand at Wembley couldn't hold his own daughter's hand in public.

That's the loneliness this story is really about.

So tell me in the comments. Do you believe the notebooks? Do you believe Freddie Mercury had a daughter and never told a soul? Or do you side with Brian May?

Thanks for sticking around until the end. Freddie would've wanted the show to go on.
