When they found Frank Fritz on his bathroom floor, nobody could have guessed what was sitting in his garage.

For more than ten years, Frank Fritz had dug through other people's barns, sheds and garages on the History Channel's American Pickers. His own private collection was worth millions. More than seventy-five vintage motorcycles, including a one-of-a-kind nineteen forty-seven Indian Chief prototype valued at two hundred fifty thousand dollars. Aerosmith's long-lost tour van. And a Star Wars Yoda prototype that George Lucas believed had been destroyed.

But the biggest surprise wasn't any of those finds. It came from a stack of handwritten journals that laid out the troubling truth about his final days, and from court records that had never been made public. Put together, they showed why American Pickers had really let him go. And who was trying to get their hands on his six-million-dollar fortune.

Frank Fritz was born in Davenport, Iowa, in nineteen sixty-three. Even as a small boy, he wasn't like the kids around him. While the other children rode bikes or traded baseball cards, Frank filled his bedroom with rocks, coins, stamps, and even empty beer cans other people had tossed out. He collected things most kids never looked at twice. His parents, Bill and Susan, saw a harmless hobby. Frank saw something in those forgotten objects that was worth keeping alive.

He remembered later that he even saved every staple he pulled out of his school papers. That's how far it went. Razor blades fascinated him too. It seemed strange at the time, but it was an early sign of his gift for seeing value in what everyone else ignored.

As he grew up, Frank went to Sudlow Intermediate and then to Bettendorf High School. That's where he met Mike Wolfe. The friendship started in eighth grade. Before long, the two boys were spending their weekends hunting through garage sales and flea markets. The same kinds of objects pulled at both of them. Odd, dusty, forgotten things that carried a history.

Some of his teachers thought Frank could do well on the traditional path. He picked a different one. He didn't think college would teach him the things he most wanted to learn. He trusted experience instead. Studying old objects, digging up their backstories, and figuring out what made them valuable.

After high school, Frank got a job as a fire inspector, covering large stretches of Iowa. He stayed in that line of work for close to twenty-five years. But collecting never let go of him. If anything, it got stronger.

He'd started buying motorcycles as a teenager. At just sixteen, he spent more than four thousand dollars on a nineteen sixty-four Harley-Davidson, a serious amount of money back then. His parents could hardly believe it. To Frank, though, the bike was a lot more than transportation. It was a link to history.

He worked hard at places like Quad City Automatic Sprinkler and Coast to Coast Hardware to pay for a hobby that kept growing. In time, he owned more than a hundred vintage motorcycles. He even built a barn just to hold them all. Every year, he rode to the Sturgis Motorcycle Rally in South Dakota, and he was a known name among bikers long before television viewers ever heard of him.

What set Frank apart went beyond what he bought. It was how he found it. He had what he called a picker's eye. In an old barn or attic, he could see potential that got past everyone else. For years, he poked around dusty properties, kept track of places that might hold something interesting, and thought about the people who had owned those things before. Just collecting an object was never enough. He wanted its story too. That outlook turned him from a casual collector into someone who treated every find as a piece of history he could pull back from the trash.

All through his years as a fire inspector, Frank kept feeding that passion. His visits to older buildings kept putting him in front of things their owners had left behind. Fire helmets, alarms, old equipment. To him, they weren't tools that had outlived their purpose. They held history, and Frank knew how to see it.

His inspections slowly took on a second purpose. Alongside the safety paperwork and the fire codes, he found chances to hunt. Over time, that odd combination, an inspector by trade and a picker by instinct, shaped the expert he'd become. Inspecting buildings sharpened his eye for detail. He learned to read a structure and notice what others walked past. Later, that skill helped him comb antique shops and storage sheds for forgotten valuables. He wasn't working on hunches. He worked by system.

Years of studying walls and spotting hazards had trained him to notice what other people missed. On American Pickers, that came in handy every time he waded through a mountain of junk looking for the one thing that was actually worth something.

One small purchase marked a turning point. Frank bought an ordinary old item for fifteen dollars and later sold it for four hundred seventy-five. The deal stuck with him. That one sale convinced him his passion could be a real business. From then on, he looked at collecting differently. A hobby, yes. But also a chance to build something bigger.

In two thousand two, Frank went all in. He left the inspection job and opened Frank's Finds in Savanna, Illinois. His place gave visitors more than somewhere to shop. It felt like a small museum packed with firefighting gear, antique tools, and things most people had forgotten existed.

Frank thought that was part of the charm. The place matched his personality. Rough around the edges, real, and full of character.

Over the years, collectors and curious travelers found their way there. People came just to look at the strange, fascinating pieces he had on display. The layout wasn't for everyone. Some visitors had a hard time getting around in there. But the people who saw the world the way Frank did understood what made it special.

That shop, and everything in it, was the foundation for what came next. When television finally knocked, Frank was ready. He didn't have to put on an act. He just had to be himself. A man who found treasure in what other people threw away, and who believed every object had a story worth hearing.

In twenty thirteen, Frank decided to pass on what he knew. He wrote How to Pick Vintage Motorcycles. He didn't write it just for experts. He wanted it to work for anyone who loved bikes or wanted to start collecting them.

The book mixed practical advice, stories from his own life, and photographs of the motorcycles he'd found. He explained how to spot a good deal, how to negotiate, and how to see the potential in something other people had passed over. His voice on the page was direct, funny and easy to follow, the same way it was on screen. With the book, Frank wanted to hand over the understanding he'd built over the years. He hoped readers would see the history inside these machines, and feel the joy he felt when an engine turned over again.

His years as a fire inspector shaped another passion too. Firehouse memorabilia. Those twenty-five years inspecting buildings got him into places most collectors never saw. Empty firehouses. Old stations. Equipment nobody used anymore. There was opportunity in all of it, for anyone who knew where to look.

Frank started collecting all he could. Brass fire extinguishers. Leather helmets. Old alarm systems. On American Pickers, you could watch his face light up any time he came across something tied to firefighting. To him, it was more than a category. It was personal.

The way he looked at a collection set him apart. He saw past the individual objects. He understood the stories inside them. Every piece held a memory of a time, a place, or a person. He often spent more time listening to an owner tell an object's story than talking about its price.

He especially valued things that showed how Americans used to live. Old tools, forgotten products, strange toys. All of it meant something. Frank's interest in history went well beyond motorcycles, though. Among the estate's belongings sat a beat-up van that looked like nothing at first glance. What made it special was what it had been. Frank and Mike Wolfe found it in the woods of Massachusetts in twenty eighteen, bought it for twenty-five thousand dollars, and restored it.
