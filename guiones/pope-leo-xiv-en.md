## INTRO

Sunday, October eleventh, twenty twenty-six. Standing before the crowd in Saint Peter's Square, Pope Leo the Fourteenth made an announcement nobody saw coming.

Two days later, he was on an operating table. And the Vatican had to tell the world what the surgeons found inside the Pope's lung.

Stay with me until the end. Because a man who spent his entire life praying for other people made a different kind of request that Sunday. He asked the world to pray for him.

But to understand the weight of that request, you have to know the man standing in that window.

And to know that man, you have to go back to a modest house in a suburb on the South Side of Chicago.

Dolton, Illinois. A working-class town of small houses and quiet streets.

That's where Robert Francis Prevost was born, in September of nineteen fifty-five. The youngest of three boys.

His father, Louis, had served in the Navy and later became a teacher and a school principal. His mother, Mildred, volunteered as a librarian at a Catholic school and never missed a parish event.

It was a house where faith was part of everything. All three boys were altar servers and went to Catholic school.

But the youngest was different.

Years later, the middle brother, John, said Robert knew what he wanted to be from the first grade on. A priest. He never considered anything else.

And this wasn't just kid talk.

Inside that house, the boy had turned an ordinary household object into an altar. And the games he played left his brothers unsure whether to laugh or take him seriously.

His mother's ironing board became the altar.

Robert would stand behind it, like a real priest, and say pretend Masses for his brothers.

The oldest brother, Louis, remembered later that there was even communion. Instead of a host, Robert used little round sugar candies.

The brothers thought it was funny. But nobody in that house doubted he meant it.

Then, one ordinary afternoon, a neighbor looked at that boy playing in the street and said something the family never forgot.

John told the story. They were small, maybe first or second grade. They were playing in front of the house when one of the mothers on the block looked at Robert and said that boy was going to be the first American pope.

Nobody made much of it. It was just something a neighbor said.

More than sixty years later, it came true.

Robert grew up with the same plan. At thirteen, fourteen, right after finishing grade school, he left home.

He went to a high school seminary run by the Augustinian priests, far from his family. From then on, he only came home for summer, Easter and Christmas.

The boy who said Mass over an ironing board had chosen his road very early. But that road was going to take him somewhere no neighbor in Dolton could have imagined. And it wasn't Rome.

## PART 1

Before he got to that place, the boy from Dolton still had a long road of study ahead of him.

At Villanova University, in Pennsylvania, he picked a major very few people expected from a future priest. Mathematics. He graduated in nineteen seventy-seven.

That same year, he joined the Order of Saint Augustine. He took his vows, studied theology in Chicago, and was sent to Rome.

In June of nineteen eighty-two, at twenty-six, Robert Prevost was ordained a priest in the same city where, more than four decades later, he would put on the white robes of the Pope.

He stayed in Rome studying Church law. Knowledge that, years later, would weigh heavily on his life.

But the first assignment for that newly ordained priest was a long way from the Vatican. In a place where, back then, being a priest could get you killed.

Nineteen eighty-five. The Augustinians sent the young priest to Peru.

Chulucanas. A poor town in the north of the country, right where the coastal desert starts climbing toward the Andes.

He showed up in blue jeans, speaking broken Spanish, as an altar boy from those days remembered later.

And he showed up at the worst possible moment.

Peru was at war with a violent guerrilla group, the Shining Path. There were bombings, attacks, and threats against anyone who got in the way. Priests were not spared.

One day, the war came to the door of the church where he worked. Literally.

An explosion blew the front door off the church.

And along with the bomb came a message for the American priests in the area. They had one day to leave. If they stayed, they would die.

A seminarian from that time, who was twenty years old, told the story years later.

The priests stayed.

According to him, what convinced those men not to leave was the people. They had walked through the communities, gotten to know the families, felt the warmth of those people. And they refused to abandon anyone.

Robert Prevost was about thirty. And he had just chosen to stay in a place where death had a scheduled date. That decision would define the rest of his life.

The years that followed were years of hard work.

He took the town's young people to the beach. He hired karate, swimming and basketball instructors to get the boys off the streets and away from crime.

He got up at five in the morning to pray. He went to bed at eleven at night. And according to the people who lived alongside him, he had a sense of humor nobody expected from such a serious priest.

In nineteen eighty-eight he was sent to Trujillo, another city in northern Peru. He spent almost ten years there.

He ran the training of new priests. He taught. And he took care of a parish in a poor neighborhood on the edge of the city, where the people called him Padre Roberto.

Years later, one of the men who worked beside him borrowed a famous expression from Pope Francis to describe him. He said he was a shepherd who smelled like the sheep. A man who lived in the middle of his people.

But while Padre Roberto was looking after poor families in Peru, his own family, back in Chicago, was starting to say goodbye. And the first loss came while he was far away.

June, nineteen ninety. Padre Roberto was in Peru when his mother, Mildred, died in Chicago.

The woman who helped out at the parish and watched her youngest turn her ironing board into an altar. She didn't live to see where that boy would end up.

Seven years later, in nineteen ninety-seven, it was his father, Louis.

Robert was forty-two. Both parents gone. And more than a decade away from home.

It was right around then that his life started changing in size. The Augustinians called Padre Roberto back. And what came next, nobody in Peru expected.

In nineteen ninety-nine, he was chosen to lead the Augustinians of Chicago. Two years later, in two thousand and one, he was elected Prior General of the Order of Saint Augustine worldwide.

The priest who had arrived in Peru in blue jeans now led thousands of religious men and women across dozens of countries.

He held the job for twelve years. He traveled the entire world, visiting Augustinian communities on every continent.

And when his term ended, in twenty thirteen, he went back to Chicago to work on the formation of young Augustinians.

But the next year, a decision came down from Rome. And it came from Pope Francis himself.

## PART 2

November, twenty fourteen. Francis sent Robert Prevost back to Peru. This time, to run an entire diocese. Chiclayo, in the north of the country.

In December, in the cathedral of Chiclayo, he was ordained a bishop.

Padre Roberto had come home.

And the following year, he did something that showed how much he loved that land. In August of twenty fifteen, he became a Peruvian citizen. The boy from Chicago was now Peruvian on paper, too.

In Chiclayo, he was a bishop who stayed close. He visited the communities and walked the outskirts of the diocese.

And it was in Chiclayo that something happened which today, after that announcement in the Vatican, carries a meaning nobody could have imagined. A story about the air people breathe.

Twenty twenty. The pandemic hit Peru with terrible force.

In the hospitals of the north, the most basic thing a COVID patient needs was running out. Oxygen.

Desperate families lined up in the street trying to buy a single tank. Many people died unable to breathe.

The bishop of Chiclayo picked up his own cell phone. He recorded a video asking for help. And he launched a campaign to buy an oxygen plant for the city.

The money came in. It was enough to install the first plant, at the Caritas headquarters in Chiclayo. And enough was left over for a second one, in a neighboring district.

The two plants started giving free oxygen to thousands of families.

Hold on to that image. The bishop who fought to get air into the lungs of Peru's sick. Because six years later, it would be his own lungs in the headlines around the world.

But before that, the oxygen bishop still had one more call coming. The call that changed everything.

January, twenty twenty-three. Pope Francis pulled Robert Prevost out of Peru once again. And brought the bishop of Chiclayo inside the Vatican.

The job was one of the most powerful in the Church. Prefect of the department that helps the Pope choose bishops for the entire world.

But before he could pack his bags, Chiclayo was going to need him one last time. And the photos from that day would travel the world years later.

March, twenty twenty-three. A cyclone named Yaku dumped torrential rain on northern Peru. Rivers overflowed and houses collapsed. Dozens of people died and thousands were left homeless.

The bishop who had already been called to Rome pulled on rubber boots and walked into the water.

In the photos, Robert Prevost is wearing a white shirt, with his bishop's cross on his chest, standing in a flood of muddy water up to his shins.

He also organized a relief campaign for the families hit by the storm, with a name that said it all. Peru lends a hand, united for Lambayeque, the region around Chiclayo.

Only after that did he leave for the Vatican.

In September of that same year, Francis made him a cardinal.

The priest in blue jeans had become one of the most influential men in Rome. And almost nobody outside the Church knew his name.

Until, in April of twenty twenty-five, the man who had brought Robert Prevost to Rome died. And the whole world started looking at the cardinals.

April twenty-first, twenty twenty-five. The Monday after Easter. Pope Francis died at eighty-eight.

Two weeks later, one hundred and thirty-three cardinals locked themselves inside the Sistine Chapel to choose his successor.

Back in Illinois, his brother John had talked to him by phone shortly before the voting. The two of them had a habit of playing a daily word game on their phones. And on that call, John joked and asked him if he'd seen the recent movie about a conclave.

Outside, reporters were drawing up lists of favorites. Names from Italy and the Philippines.

Cardinal Prevost's name barely showed up on those lists. For a simple reason. He was American. And the experts kept repeating an unwritten rule: the most powerful country on the planet would not get the Pope too.

But inside that chapel, something was happening that no list of favorites could have predicted.

May eighth, twenty twenty-five. Day two of the conclave.

At eight minutes past six in the evening, Rome time, smoke rose from the chimney of the Sistine Chapel.

White.

Saint Peter's Square erupted. Thousands of people screamed and cried. Nobody knew yet who had been chosen.

A little over an hour later, the curtains on the central balcony of the Basilica opened. And the announcement came in Latin.

The new Pope was Cardinal Robert Francis Prevost. And he had chosen the name Leo the Fourteenth.

The first Pope born in the United States. The first Augustinian in history.

In Illinois, John turned on the television and called his niece over. When her uncle's name appeared, she started screaming. He just stood there, unable to believe it.

And when he finally stepped out onto the balcony, he still had a surprise in store for an entire country on the other side of the ocean.

The man in white looked out at the crowd and wished everyone peace.

Then, in the middle of his speech, he switched to Spanish. And sent a special greeting to his beloved diocese of Chiclayo, in Peru.

In Chiclayo, people poured into the streets to celebrate. Their Padre Roberto was the Pope.

That night, the words of that neighbor in Dolton, spoken to a six- or seven-year-old boy, had finally come true.

The first American pope.

Two days later, in front of the cardinals, he explained the name he had chosen.

The last Pope to carry it was Leo the Thirteenth, at the end of the nineteenth century. He was the one who wrote a famous document defending workers, at a time when factories were changing the world.

Leo the Fourteenth said the world is living through a similar revolution today. Artificial intelligence. And that the Church must once again defend the dignity of people and of work.

But nobody in that square imagined that, one year and five months later, that same man would appear in a Vatican window to make a completely different kind of announcement.

## PART 3

Sunday, October eleventh, twenty twenty-six. Noon.

As he does every Sunday, Leo the Fourteenth appeared before the faithful for the Angelus prayer. The square was full. Nobody expected anything out of the ordinary.

Then the Pope made a personal announcement.

He said that in recent medical tests, doctors had found a small nodule in his lung. He said it had been caught very early and was localized. And he announced that he had decided to have surgery.

Then he thanked people for their affection. And he entrusted himself to their prayers.

Two days later, he walked into the hospital on his own two feet, through the front door. And waved to the people waiting outside.

Tuesday, October thirteenth. Gemelli Hospital, in Rome. The same hospital where other popes had been treated.

John Paul the Second was there so many times that he called the Gemelli his third Vatican. And it was there that Francis spent more than five weeks with pneumonia, just months before he died.

Leo the Fourteenth was seventy-one.

He was in the operating suite for about three hours. The surgery itself lasted roughly an hour and a half, led by the hospital's chief of thoracic surgery.

The surgeons removed an entire section of his lung. The right lung has three parts, called lobes. They took out the bottom one, the lower lobe.

After the operation, the Vatican released a medical bulletin.

The surgery had gone well. No complications.

And then came the line the whole world read in the headlines.

The tests performed after the operation confirmed the diagnosis. Lung adenocarcinoma.

Cancer.

It's the most common type of lung cancer. It starts in the cells that produce mucus, in the outer parts of the lung. It often grows without a single symptom, which is why, in most cases, it's only discovered late.

In the Pope's case, it was the opposite. The nodule was found early. And according to doctors quoted in the press before the surgery, when a tumor like this is removed at that stage, the odds are very good.

People started answering the request he had made on Sunday.

At the main entrance to the Gemelli there's a statue of John Paul the Second. Beneath it, a line. Be not afraid.

The day after the surgery, the feet of that statue were covered in lit candles and prayer cards. Strangers stopped there to pray for the Pope. Some knelt on the ground.

And for anyone who knows his story, that news brought back a memory from Chiclayo. The bishop who, six years earlier, had scrambled to find oxygen for the sick.

The Pope who recorded a video begging for help so other people could breathe was now in a hospital room, doing physical therapy to get his own breath back.

Within days, he was walking the hallway. He was eating normally again. According to the Vatican, he was fully independent, moving and breathing on his own.

On the sixteenth, he celebrated Mass right there, on the tenth floor of the hospital. It was the first time the world saw photos of him after the operation.

And while still in the hospital, he made a phone call nobody expected. He spoke by telephone with the president of Russia, Vladimir Putin.

But to understand everything this man carries today, you have to go back to a much older story. A story that has followed Robert Prevost since his days in Chiclayo. And one he has never been able to fully leave behind.

## PART 4

April, twenty twenty-two. Chiclayo, in northern Peru.

Three sisters came to the diocese Robert Prevost ran as bishop. They brought a very serious accusation against a priest in the region.

They said they had been abused by that priest when they were still girls. Years earlier, inside the Church itself.

One of them is named Ana María Quispe. And she's the one who later decided to tell the story publicly.

According to the sisters, the complaint didn't move the way it should have. They say they were never called in to give a statement. They say they never received a single document showing a real investigation was underway. And they say the accused priest kept celebrating Mass.

To them, the bishop of that diocese had failed.

And that accusation stayed buried for a long time. Until the day Robert Prevost's name was read from the balcony of Saint Peter's.

When he became Pope, the story of the Chiclayo sisters went around the world. It ran in newspapers everywhere. And a lot of people who had just been moved to tears by that man on the balcony started asking the same question.

What did he actually do when he received that complaint?

The diocese of Chiclayo answered with a different version.

According to the diocese, the complaint was received in the first week of April, twenty twenty-two. The bishop opened a preliminary investigation, as Church rules require. And the case was sent to Rome, to the Vatican office that handles these matters.

On the Peruvian side, the courts looked at the case too. And prosecutors closed it in twenty twenty-three, because the events had happened too long ago and the statute of limitations had run out.

That same year, according to the diocese, the Vatican also closed its investigation.

The bishop who took over Chiclayo after him came to Prevost's defense. He said Prevost listened to the victims and followed every step of the process.

But the strongest defense came from where very few people expected.

Peruvian journalist Pedro Salinas spent years exposing abuse inside a powerful Catholic group in Peru, the Sodalitium. He was a victim of that group himself. And for a long time, part of the Church treated him as an enemy.

Well, he's the one who defended the bishop of Chiclayo.

According to Pedro Salinas, Prevost had stood with the Sodalitium's victims when very few bishops had the courage to. And in his view, the accusations against the bishop came from people tied to that group, who wanted to hurt the man who had helped take it on.

That's one journalist's assessment. The Chiclayo sisters stand by their account.

And this is where the story gets hard to resolve. On one side, three women who say the Church abandoned them. On the other, a diocese that says it followed every step. And Leo the Fourteenth will carry that question for the rest of his life.

Nobody knows whether he was thinking about it in that hospital room. But a few weeks before the surgery, there was a meeting that says a lot about the kind of Pope he decided to be.

September twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-six. Lourdes, France.

The Pope was at the shrine where, for generations, sick people from all over the world have traveled in search of healing.

That day, he celebrated Mass. And he asked the Church to have the humility to look its own wounds in the face.

Hours later, he walked into a closed room. Inside, seven people were waiting for him. All of them had been abused by priests in France.

The conversation lasted two hours. According to the Vatican, the Pope received each of them privately. He listened to each one's story. And he listened to what they proposed so the Church could better protect children.

When they came out, the survivors said they felt heard. But they made clear they expect those words to turn into action. One of them, Jérôme Guillement, said the Church in France still has a lot to do.

And that meeting wasn't the first. In June, on a trip to Spain, he had already received six survivors in Madrid. He spent almost an hour listening to each of them.

That day, nobody imagined that, a little over two weeks later, he would be the one walking into a hospital.

## PART 5

Today, the Pope is recovering at the Gemelli. And according to the Vatican, the recovery is going the way the doctors expected. All audiences have been canceled through October twentieth.

But there's one date on his calendar nobody has forgotten.

November sixth.

That's the day a long trip through South America is scheduled to begin. And one of the stops is Peru.

The land he arrived in as a young priest. Where he found a bomb at the church door and decided to stay. And where, years later, as a bishop, he scrambled to find oxygen for the sick.

The Vatican hasn't confirmed yet whether the trip will happen. After surgery like this, doctors usually recommend about three weeks without flying. And November sixth falls right at the edge of that window.

Even so, the Vatican has already released the list of journalists who will travel on the Pope's plane.

And if he does manage to board, he'll go back to Peru carrying everything he lived there. The good memories, and the question that still hurts in Chiclayo.

A boy from Dolton who turned his mother's ironing board into an altar. A priest who didn't leave Chulucanas when violence came to the door. A bishop who recorded a video begging for oxygen for people who couldn't breathe. And a Pope who, at seventy-one, heard from his doctors the word nobody wants to hear.

That's the man who appeared in the square that Sunday.

He spent his whole life praying for others. He prayed for the poor of Peru. He prayed for the sick during the pandemic.

And that Sunday, he made a different request. He asked the world to pray for him.

Because in front of a medical test, nobody is the Pope. Everybody becomes just a person sitting in a room, waiting for the doctor to speak.

Leo the Fourteenth's cancer was caught early because he got the tests done. A lot of people find this same kind of cancer far too late, because it grows in silence.

So if you've been putting off that checkup, this story is for you too.

And the biggest lesson this man left behind that Sunday fits in one simple sentence. Even someone who spent a lifetime taking care of others, one day, has to find the courage to ask to be taken care of.
