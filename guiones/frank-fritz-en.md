## INTRO

When they found Frank Fritz on his bathroom floor, nobody could have guessed what was sitting in his garage.

For more than ten years, Frank Fritz had dug through other people's barns, sheds and garages on the History Channel's American Pickers. His own private collection was worth millions. More than seventy-five vintage motorcycles, including a one-of-a-kind nineteen forty-seven Indian Chief prototype valued at two hundred fifty thousand dollars. Aerosmith's long-lost tour van. And a Star Wars Yoda prototype that George Lucas believed had been destroyed.

But the biggest surprise wasn't any of those finds. It came from a stack of handwritten journals that laid out the troubling truth about his final days, and from court records that had never been made public. Put together, they showed why American Pickers had really let him go. And who was trying to get their hands on his six-million-dollar fortune.

## PART 1

Frank Fritz was born in Davenport, Iowa, in nineteen sixty-three. Even as a small boy, he wasn't like the kids around him. While the other children rode bikes or traded baseball cards, Frank filled his bedroom with rocks, coins, stamps, and even empty beer cans other people had tossed out. He collected things most kids never looked at twice. His parents, Bill and Susan, saw a harmless hobby. Frank saw something in those forgotten objects that was worth keeping alive.

He remembered later that he even saved every staple he pulled out of his school papers. That's how far it went. Razor blades fascinated him too. It seemed strange at the time, but it was an early sign of his gift for seeing value in what everyone else ignored.

As he grew up, Frank went to Sudlow Intermediate and then to Bettendorf High School. That's where he met Mike Wolfe. The friendship started in eighth grade. Before long, the two boys were spending their weekends hunting through garage sales and flea markets. The same kinds of objects pulled at both of them. Odd, dusty, forgotten things that carried a history.

Some of his teachers thought Frank could do well on the traditional path. He picked a different one. He didn't think college would teach him the things he most wanted to learn. He trusted experience instead. Studying old objects, digging up their backstories, and figuring out what made them valuable.

After high school, Frank got a job as a fire inspector, covering large stretches of Iowa. He stayed in that line of work for close to twenty-five years. But collecting never let go of him. If anything, it got stronger.

He'd started buying motorcycles as a teenager. At just sixteen, he spent more than four thousand dollars on a nineteen sixty-four Harley-Davidson, a serious amount of money back then. His parents could hardly believe it. To Frank, though, the bike was a lot more than transportation. It was a link to history.

He worked hard at places like Quad City Automatic Sprinkler and Coast to Coast Hardware to pay for a hobby that kept growing. In time, he owned more than a hundred vintage motorcycles. He even built a barn just to hold them all. Every year, he rode to the Sturgis Motorcycle Rally in South Dakota, and he was a known name among bikers long before television viewers ever heard of him.

What set Frank apart went beyond what he bought. It was how he found it. He had what he called a picker's eye. In an old barn or attic, he could see potential that got past everyone else. For years, he poked around dusty properties, kept track of places that might hold something interesting, and thought about the people who had owned those things before. Just collecting an object was never enough. He wanted its story too. That outlook turned him from a casual collector into someone who treated every find as a piece of history he could pull back from the trash.

All through his years as a fire inspector, Frank kept feeding that passion. His visits to older buildings kept putting him in front of things their owners had left behind. Fire helmets, alarms, old equipment. To him, they weren't tools that had outlived their purpose. They held history, and Frank knew how to see it.

His inspections slowly took on a second purpose. Alongside the safety paperwork and the fire codes, he found chances to hunt. Over time, that odd combination, an inspector by trade and a picker by instinct, shaped the expert he'd become. Inspecting buildings sharpened his eye for detail. He learned to read a structure and notice what others walked past. Later, that skill helped him comb antique shops and storage sheds for forgotten valuables. He wasn't working on hunches. He worked by system.

## PART 2

Years of studying walls and spotting hazards had trained him to notice what other people missed. On American Pickers, that came in handy every time he waded through a mountain of junk looking for the one thing that was actually worth something.

One small purchase marked a turning point. Frank bought an ordinary old item for fifteen dollars and later sold it for four hundred seventy-five. The deal stuck with him. It wasn't just the nice return. It was the realization that everything he'd learned had a dollar value. All those years of paying attention to these objects were starting to pay off. That one sale convinced him his passion could be a real business. From then on, he looked at collecting differently. A hobby, yes. But also a chance to build something bigger.

In two thousand two, Frank went all in. He left the inspection job and opened Frank's Finds in Savanna, Illinois. His place gave visitors more than somewhere to shop. It felt like a small museum packed with firefighting gear, antique tools, and things most people had forgotten existed.

The building had a history of its own. The store sat in an old opera house next to a bar, and walking in felt like walking into another decade. Some visitors found it dark and musty. Frank thought that was part of the charm. The place matched his personality. Rough around the edges, real, and full of character.

Over the years, collectors and curious travelers found their way there. People came just to look at the strange, fascinating pieces he had on display. The layout wasn't for everyone. Some visitors had a hard time getting around in there. But the people who saw the world the way Frank did understood what made it special.

That shop, and everything in it, was the foundation for what came next. When television finally knocked, Frank was ready. He didn't have to put on an act. He just had to be himself. A man who found treasure in what other people threw away, and who believed every object had a story worth hearing.

## PART 3

Before American Pickers was a hit, Mike Wolfe was already out hunting old stuff with a camera in his hand. He started filming his trips around two thousand two. At the same time, his old friend Frank Fritz had just traded a steady career in fire inspection for an antique store in Savanna, Illinois. Frank liked running the shop, surrounded by vintage motorcycles, oil cans and old toys. He had no plans to hit the road. Mike had bigger plans for both of them.

Mike believed their picking trips could turn into something, and he spent months trying to talk Frank into joining him. Frank kept saying no. He liked his store. It gave him security. It was his.

Then one purchase changed his mind. Frank bought a small fraternal lodge badge for one hundred fifteen dollars. It didn't look like much. Later, he sold it for one thousand four hundred fifty. The sale gave him a rush he compared to a gambler's high. That feeling, plus Mike's constant pushing, finally got him to give it a shot.

He closed the shop, packed his gear, and hit the road with Mike. Those first loosely filmed trips eventually produced the footage that got television producers' attention.

American Pickers premiered in January of twenty ten, and it changed both their lives. Frank went from a quiet shop owner to a man millions of people welcomed into their living rooms every week. The first episode drew more than three million viewers, one of the strongest debuts in the History Channel's history.

Frank's beard, his solid build, and his slow, easy way of talking balanced Mike's high energy perfectly. Viewers loved the contrast. Soon fans were lining up to meet them. When they visited the California Surf Museum that October, a huge crowd gathered outside. Frank, confused, asked what had brought all those people there. Then he stayed and signed autographs until every single one of them had one.

Just eight months after the show started, one episode pulled in five point three million viewers. Frank Fritz was a television star.

## PART 4

In the early days, their trips were adventures with no set route. They drove a white Mercedes van, later a Ford Transit, and followed no schedule.

In September of twenty eleven, they rolled through southern Maine and came across Champion Auto in Arundel. The owner, Mario Bernett, could barely believe it. He watched the show every week, and now its two stars were standing in his yard. Frank bought oil cans, signs and gas station collectibles. Mike went for the motorcycle parts. They spent five days in the area chasing leads and meeting people, with no script.

That simple experience was the heart of the show's appeal. Two friends in a van, following the leftovers of another time.

Part of the show's popularity came from how differently the two men were built. Mike, tall and lean, ran on excitement. Frank, shorter, took a quieter, more methodical approach. Mike hunted old bicycles and Volkswagens. Frank went for toys, oil cans, and anything connected to motorcycles. You could see it at Mario's place in Maine. Mike pulled out the motorcycle pieces. Frank picked the gas station signs. Their tastes fit together like two halves of a puzzle.

Mike once called Frank a dreamer, and described him as sensitive, funny and thoughtful. Audiences saw the same thing, and they loved the bond between two men who had so little in common on the surface. Some fans related to Frank's slower pace. Others clicked with Mike's speed. Together, those differences gave people a way in.

Frank's love of motorcycles went back long before television. As a teenager, he bought a nineteen sixty-four Harley-Davidson, a purchase that probably gave his parents a few gray hairs. That first bike led to many more. Over time, he put together about thirty-five vintage motorcycles in a barn that looked a lot like a museum.

The collection included rare Indians, among them a nineteen thirty-eight four-cylinder he found in a barn in Philadelphia. It included British brands too. Vincent. BSA. Triumph. One bike he prized above the others was the Von Dutch XAVW, a nineteen forty-two Harley frame paired with a Volkswagen engine and paintwork by Von Dutch himself. Frank had admired that machine in a magazine decades before he stumbled onto it at a yard sale, which made finding it all the sweeter. Like so many bikes in his care, it had a history. And the history was what mattered most to him.

## PART 5

In twenty thirteen, Frank decided to pass on what he knew. He wrote How to Pick Vintage Motorcycles. He didn't write it just for experts. He wanted it to work for anyone who loved bikes or wanted to start collecting them.

The book mixed practical advice, stories from his own life, and photographs of the motorcycles he'd found. He explained how to spot a good deal, how to negotiate, and how to see the potential in something other people had passed over. His voice on the page was direct, funny and easy to follow, the same way it was on screen. With the book, Frank wanted to hand over the understanding he'd built over the years. He hoped readers would see the history inside these machines, and feel the joy he felt when an engine turned over again.

His years as a fire inspector shaped another passion too. Firehouse memorabilia. Those twenty-five years inspecting buildings got him into places most collectors never saw. Empty firehouses. Old stations. Equipment nobody used anymore. There was opportunity in all of it, for anyone who knew where to look.

Frank started collecting all he could. Brass fire extinguishers. Leather helmets. Old alarm systems. On American Pickers, you could watch his face light up any time he came across something tied to firefighting. To him, it was more than a category. It was personal.

The way he looked at a collection set him apart. He saw past the individual objects. He understood the stories inside them. Every piece held a memory of a time, a place, or a person. He often spent more time listening to an owner tell an object's story than talking about its price.

He especially valued things that showed how Americans used to live. Old tools, forgotten products, strange toys. All of it meant something. He treated wartime items with deep respect, and more than once he donated things connected to fallen service members to museums. That was who Frank was. He believed people should protect those stories, not lock them away.

## PART 6

For most of his life, Frank Fritz was fighting a battle almost nobody could see. He lived with Crohn's disease for more than thirty-six years. A painful, draining illness that made ordinary life hard, and made filming American Pickers harder.

A close friend said later that the trouble started when Frank was around twenty-five or thirty. He hid it well. He worked through the pain while millions watched him on television with no idea what he was going through. Quitting drinking and committing to sobriety helped for a while. But years of illness and pressure had already done their damage. His health kept sliding until his body couldn't carry the weight anymore, and he died at sixty. Many people still had no idea how much he'd suffered in private.

Some of his troubles went public in twenty seventeen. Police pulled Frank over after he drove on the wrong side of the road. He admitted he'd been drinking and had taken Xanax, which he said he had a prescription for, to manage anxiety. He failed a sobriety test and was charged with operating while intoxicated.

Later, he tied his anxiety to the feeling of being watched and judged all the time. Frank said people picked apart everything he did, and eventually the pressure got to be too much. He avoided jail with a plea deal, but the damage to his image was done. The episode showed how fame had started to grind him down and change his life.

His friendship with his American Pickers co-star Mike Wolfe was coming apart too. By twenty twenty-one, Frank said the two of them hadn't spoken in two years. That silence hurt him more than losing the work. Mike hadn't called after Frank's back surgery.

Frank felt their places on the show had never been equal. With Mike in the spotlight and Frank just along for the ride, he joked that it was like being in a band with Steven Tyler. He was never going to be the lead singer.

When Mike posted online that he missed Frank, Frank didn't buy it. He called the post phony and suggested somebody else had probably written it. He wondered whether Mike had ever said a kind word about him in private. Still, after years of tension, the two met again in May of twenty twenty-three, over Memorial Day weekend. A mutual friend brought them together.

But the other problems hadn't gone anywhere. During the pandemic, Frank hurt his back moving something at home. He needed surgery at the worst possible time. Pandemic lockdowns, isolation, and the end of a relationship all hit at once. In those months, he got hooked on opioids. The pills eased the pain at first. Before long, he'd lost control of them.

Mike Wolfe compared it to watching a storm tear someone apart. The people close to Frank tried to help. They held an intervention. They begged him to get sober. Frank insisted he didn't have a problem, even as everything around him said otherwise.

When the show looked at bringing him back, it asked him for a clean drug test. He couldn't produce one. That was the final blow to his future on American Pickers.

His last episode aired in March of twenty twenty. At first, viewers took his absence as a short break to recover. Frank later insisted he had never left the show on his own. He'd had back surgery, and then COVID hit. But by July of twenty twenty-one, Mike confirmed that Frank would not be coming back. The announcement wished him well. It explained very little.

## PART 7

Behind the scenes, it was more complicated. Frank had been through a major operation, one hundred eighty-five stitches and two rods in his spine. He said he'd healed completely, that he was ready, and that he wanted to go back to work. The network had other ideas. It decided against bringing him back.

People who knew Frank said the decision crushed him. He felt pushed aside and replaced. His health got worse after that.

On July fourteenth, twenty twenty-two, his life changed all over again. That morning, a friend walked into his Iowa farmhouse and found him lying on the floor. He wasn't moving. He couldn't talk. The friend called nine one one right away. I think he might be having a seizure, the caller said. He can't speak to me.

Frank was fifty-nine, and he had suffered a massive stroke. Doctors rushed him to the hospital, but his brain had already taken heavy damage. He lost much of his ability to move and to think clearly. After a month in the hospital, he was moved to a nursing home. And he was only at the start of a long, agonizing fight to stay alive.

Even after he eventually went home, he needed help around the clock. His condition kept getting worse. There were more emergencies, more calls for help, including a bout of pneumonia that put him back in the hospital.

Over the following weeks, the people around him came to understand that Frank could no longer make decisions about his own life or his money. In August of twenty twenty-two, the court stepped in. It appointed his friend Chris Davis to make personal decisions for him. A bank took control of an estate worth about six million dollars.

The court paperwork put it plainly. Frank could not care for himself. He could not secure food, housing, or even the medical care he needed. Without help, he was at risk of serious harm or death.

But getting help brought its own complications. The court watched both his guardian and the bank. Judges reviewed what they did every year to make sure Frank's care was meeting his needs.

## PART 8

Money became another source of pressure. American Pickers had made him millions, but his savings started shrinking fast. His medical bills ran about thirty thousand dollars a month. Over a year, that passed three hundred fifty thousand.

By then, investments were his only income, about sixty thousand dollars a year. That didn't come close to covering it. He needed round-the-clock nursing, special equipment, physical therapy, and treatment for his other serious conditions, including Crohn's disease and COPD.

His close friend Jerry Gendro said there had even been talk of auctioning off Frank's antiques after he died. I think an auction could do extremely well for him, Jerry said. He believed Frank's fans would show up for him.

As time went on, Frank got weaker. Jerry said visiting him was heartbreaking. One side of Frank's body didn't work at all anymore. He remembered watching Frank try to lift an arm, only to see it drop back down. What made it worse was that Frank understood his own condition. He knew how much he'd lost. He couldn't talk the way he used to. He couldn't move the way he wanted. And there was nothing he could do about it.

Jerry believed Frank had lost the will to keep fighting. Exhaustion, he said, wears away a person's desire to go on. I honestly think he might have lived longer if he had kept fighting.

Then the final call came. On September thirtieth, twenty twenty-four, someone told Mike Wolfe, Frank's longtime partner on American Pickers, that Frank was fading fast. Mike rushed to his side. He stayed for an hour, holding Frank's hand, rubbing his chest, talking to him in a soft voice.

I told him I wasn't mad at him, Mike remembered. I told him I loved him.

Not long before Frank took his last breath, Mike said one last thing to him. Go find your mother. Go to her now.

Frank died at eight fifty that night. Mike was still there. So was Annette Oberlander, his late mother's best friend. Annette said later that Mike had been there for Frank the whole way. He stayed to the end. She said those last moments were exactly what both men would have wanted.

## PART 9

When Frank Fritz died in September of twenty twenty-four, nobody expected what people would find in his old garage. According to the reports that followed, something remarkable had been sitting there, out of sight, for years.

Inside were more than seventy-five vintage motorcycles, each one with its own past and its own story. A few looked like they had just rolled off the factory floor. Others wore layers of dust and rust, like they were waiting for one more chance to run. The oldest went back to nineteen twelve. Several were rare prototypes that experts had assumed were gone for good.

Most people never knew Frank was quietly building a collection like that. Only a handful of trusted friends knew how much was in there. When the news got out, collectors and museums around the world lit up. Many believed it might be one of the most important motorcycle finds in decades.

A closer look turned up even more. One bike, a nineteen twenty-two Ace Four, had cost just forty-five thousand dollars during a memorable episode of American Pickers. Its estimated value had climbed to nearly two hundred thousand. Another standout was a nineteen thirty-nine Indian Four known as the Aristocrat, worth more than one hundred fifteen thousand. There were pre-war Indian Scouts, including a nineteen twenty-eight model still wearing its original factory paint. And Frank had three Harley-Davidson Panheads from the nineteen fifties, each needing a different level of restoration.

The most extraordinary piece was a one-of-a-kind nineteen forty-seven Indian Chief prototype. No other recorded model shared its design, and experts believed it could bring more than two hundred fifty thousand dollars.

But the motorcycles were only part of what made the collection so unusual. Beside them sat dozens of handwritten journals Frank had kept carefully for more than thirty years. He wrote down everything. Where each bike had come from. Who had owned it before him. Which replacement parts he still needed to track down. He wrote about hard restorations, the hours spent in his shop, and the thrill of hearing an engine come back to life. Now and then, he added a few lines about how a ride made him feel, or what memory it brought back.

Those journals gave the collection something beyond the machines. They brought its history to life. For collectors, that background was one more reason it was worth so much.

Once experts finished cataloging the bikes, they estimated the collection could be worth more than two million dollars. Some believed competitive bidding could push it past three million, especially with every piece tied to Frank's name. Auction houses competed for the chance to run the sale. Buyers knew they'd be getting more than old motorcycles. These were pieces of American history, protected by a man who understood what they meant.

## PART 10

Frank's interest in history went well beyond motorcycles, though. Among the estate's belongings sat a beat-up van that looked like nothing at first glance. What made it special was what it had been. Aerosmith had ridden in that exact vehicle in the band's earliest days, driving from gig to gig before anyone knew their name.

Frank and Mike Wolfe found it in the woods of Massachusetts in twenty eighteen, bought it for twenty-five thousand dollars, and restored it. When the band saw the van again in twenty nineteen, they were stunned. Joe Perry called it incredible. Original member Ray Tabano got emotional, remembering how they used to split the few dollars left after gas and tolls. That van had carried their instruments and their dreams.

There were other unconventional finds too. Frank once paid nine thousand five hundred dollars for an enormous elephant head from a taxidermy collector. Jack White of the White Stripes later took it off his hands in exchange for antiques and cash, partly because of his famous album, Elephant. Animal rights groups criticized the purchase, but it showed Frank's willingness to go after things other collectors wouldn't touch.

He also owned an original Jell-O wagon from the early nineteen hundreds, one that had once rolled through small towns advertising the dessert. That wagon ended up in a museum too. Music, food, strange Americana. Frank saw meaning in all of it.

He had a soft spot for science fiction collectibles as well. In one episode of American Pickers, he and Mike came across four early prototypes of the Star Wars character Yoda. At first, the figures looked like something a fan had made at home. But a former Lucasfilm artist confirmed they were real. They reflected early designs from nineteen seventy-eight, cast from molds inspired by Albert Einstein. Frank paid a serious sum for them, and eventually each one was valued at more than six thousand dollars.

That find showed how far his taste reached beyond motorcycles and signs. He understood the particular pull of popular culture.

## PART 11

But while people honored what he'd left behind, a fight broke out over his estate. In February of twenty twenty-five, Frank's father, William Fritz, known as Bill, filed a lawsuit. He claimed the will presented in twenty twenty-three was a fraud. According to Bill, Frank never signed it. The document left almost all of the six-million-dollar estate to friends and caregivers. It left Bill nothing.

He said he was Frank's only true family and his sole surviving relative, and he argued the entire estate should be his. His lawsuit asked for a jury trial to settle it.

More questions soon piled onto the case. Shortly after Frank's devastating stroke in twenty twenty-two, a conservatorship had been put in place. A judge had ruled then that Frank could not make his own decisions. Bill made that ruling the center of his argument. If Frank couldn't take care of himself, how could he legally sign a will?

Bill also claimed that other people had kept him from seeing his son in those last years. He talked about a bond that had once been close, remembered fishing trips together in St. Thomas, and said he'd been wrongly shut out. The courts will have to decide which claims the evidence supports. As more comes out, people are still waiting to see how this chapter of Frank Fritz's story ends.

One thing is already certain. Frank left behind far more than antiques and motorcycles. His legacy was built out of passion, memory, and a lifelong love of history. In a rusting engine or an old van, he saw stories worth saving.

Now the world is uncovering those stories, looking past the forgotten objects to the enthusiasm, the curiosity, and the memories that made Frank Fritz who he was. His years on American Pickers showed us that the greatest treasures sometimes have nothing to do with price, and everything to do with the people and the stories attached to them. Questions may hang over his final chapter. But the memories he made, and the joy he brought to collectors and viewers, will outlast them.
