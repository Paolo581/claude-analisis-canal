For twenty-nine years, the whole world believed the same story. Princess Diana was killed in a Paris tunnel, because her driver was drunk and going way too fast. Her two boys grew up with it, and so did the rest of us.

But it wasn't an accident. And the proof was hiding inside Diana's own autopsy the whole time, on one page nobody was ever supposed to see.

Earlier this month, a thick envelope showed up at Scotland Yard, with no name on it and no return address. Inside were twenty-two yellowed pages, stamped by a London morgue. It was Diana's complete autopsy. Until now, the courts had only heard little pieces of it.

On page nine, in the margin, there's one sentence. The doctor who examined Diana's body wrote it by hand, in blue ink. Then somebody else dragged a thick black marker across it, pressing so hard it ripped right through the paper. The report went to court with the line blacked out. In two thousand eight, the jury deciding how Diana died never got to read those words.

Now, because of this envelope, Diana's case is open again. Investigators are going back over her autopsy, line by line. And in a lab, under a special light, a few words showed through the black ink. The doctor had found a mark on Diana's body. No car crash could have made it.

But one man read the whole sentence. He worked nights at the morgue, and he read it alone in the records room, before anyone covered it. Days later, he saw it crossed out, and fear got hold of him. So he slipped the pages out of the morgue and locked them in a drawer at home. He never told a soul, not even his wife. This summer, he got very sick. From a hospital bed, with shaking hands, he asked his daughter to mail the envelope.

Today, we're going to open this autopsy together. You'll find out what the mark was, and who wanted the doctor's words buried under black ink. And at the end, you'll hear exactly what the man from the morgue read that night.

On Saturday, August thirtieth, nineteen ninety-seven, just hours before she died, Diana was happy. She picked up the phone and called her boys, William and Harry, fifteen and twelve years old. The call was short, because the boys were busy playing and wanted to hang up. They never heard their mother's voice again.

A little after midnight, the car she was riding in smashed into a concrete pillar inside the tunnel. The front end crumpled like a tin can. Diana was still alive when they pulled her out. Doctors cut open her chest and worked on her heart with their own hands for two long hours. She died at four in the morning. She was only thirty-six.

By Sunday evening, her body was back in London, laid on a cold steel table. There, the doctor examined her and wrote the line on page nine.

Somebody wanted those words hidden forever. To understand why, we have to go back two years before the crash, to a lawyer's office in London. Diana leaned across the desk and whispered that someone had warned her. Her car could be made to crash, and she could die or end up paralyzed. Her lawyer kept his head down and wrote every word.

The woman lying on that steel table in Fulham, on the night of Sunday, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven, had been born thirty-six years earlier, in a bedroom at Park House. It was a big stone house on the royal estate at Sandringham, in Norfolk, and on Saturday, July first, nineteen sixty-one, Diana Frances Spencer was born there.

Her father, John Spencer, and her mother, Frances, badly wanted a boy. A year and a half earlier, they had buried a son, also named John, who lived only ten hours. When the midwife announced it was another girl, the house greeted Diana with more silence than celebration.

She grew up feeling she had arrived in someone else's place, and that feeling of being extra, of always standing one step outside, would be exactly the door the most dangerous man in her life would walk through.

In nineteen sixty-seven, when Diana was six, her mother left Park House. Diana said later that she sat on the bottom step of the staircase, listening to the sound of the car's wheels on the gravel driveway, farther and farther away. She waited on that staircase all afternoon.

That night, her little brother Charles, three years old, cried in the next room, calling for his mother. Diana heard all of it, lying in the dark, too scared to get up and cross the hallway. She carried the guilt of those nights for years.

In nineteen sixty-nine, her father won custody of the children in court. Her mother could only see them on certain weekends. The little girl learned to listen behind doors, to read the faces of adults, and to distrust anyone who smiled too much.

That little boy crying for his mother in the next room, Charles Spencer, would grow up, inherit his father's title, and, twenty-eight years later, open the door of his own home to the man who would destroy his sister's peace.

In nineteen seventy-five, her father became an earl, and the family moved to Althorp, an enormous mansion out in the countryside, in Northamptonshire. Diana was fourteen and had a simple dream, one she repeated to her friends at school: to marry for love and have lots of children.

Althorp is the house where everything started going wrong for her, and it's also the land where she would be buried, on an island in the middle of a lake.

The rest of the world knows. On Wednesday, July twenty-ninth, nineteen eighty-one, at twenty years old, Diana walked down the nave of St Paul's Cathedral in London, in a dress with a train twenty-five feet long, in front of seven hundred and fifty million people watching on television. At the altar, she was so nervous she mixed up the order of her groom's names, Prince Charles.

On Monday, June twenty-first, nineteen eighty-two, William was born, in a hospital in Paddington. Two years later, on Saturday, September fifteenth, nineteen eighty-four, Harry was born. Diana insisted on taking the boys to school herself, eating hamburgers with them, and riding roller coasters, things no princess had done before.

It was those two boys, fifteen and twelve, she would call on the phone one last time from Paris, and it's those two who still carry the question of what their mother's body showed on that table in Fulham.

Inside, the marriage was already broken. Her husband went on loving another woman, Camilla Parker Bowles, and Diana, alone in the palace hallways, made herself throw up in secret after meals. In December nineteen ninety-two, the prime minister announced the couple's separation in Parliament.

After that, secretly recorded phone calls of hers ended up in the newspapers. Diana came to believe the phones at Kensington Palace were tapped, that the staff reported everything, that someone was following her car around the city. It was the old fear of the girl on the staircase, now with a real reason behind it.

And there was a man in London who knew exactly where that fear hurt, and who was willing to lie to make it grow.

His name was Martin Bashir. He was thirty-two, a reporter for Panorama, the most respected news program on the BBC, and he wanted one thing only: the interview every journalist in the world wanted, Diana talking, on camera, about what she had gone through inside the royal family.

To get to the princess, Bashir went through her brother. On Thursday, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-five, he drove to Althorp to meet Charles Spencer, who was thirty-one.

It was August thirty-first. Exactly two years from that day, Diana would die.

The two men sat down in a room of the mansion. Bashir opened an envelope and spread a stack of bank statements across the table. He explained, in a low voice, that those sheets showed deposits into the account of Alan Waller, a former security employee of Charles Spencer himself.

The money, according to Bashir, came from a company tied to a newspaper group and from another firm nobody had heard of. The message was clear: someone was paying people on the inside to spy on the Spencer family. Charles Spencer looked at the numbers, the dates, the stamps, and believed every line.

Those statements were fake.

Bashir had asked a graphic designer who worked for the BBC, Matt Wiessler, to mock up documents like that on a computer. Wiessler did what the reporter asked, thinking it was material for a story. The accounts, the deposits, and the names on those sheets, Bashir made up at an office desk, to fool a brother who was afraid for his sister. Twenty-six years later, in May twenty twenty-one, a retired judge, Lord Dyson, spelled that out in an official report.

Charles Spencer would later say a single sentence, one he still carries like a stone on his chest.

If I hadn't seen those statements, I would never have introduced Martin Bashir to my sister.

But he did see them. And that same afternoon at Althorp, convinced, he promised to set up a meeting between the reporter and Diana in London. Bashir put the papers back in the envelope, said thank you, and left down the same gravel driveway where, almost thirty years earlier, his mother's car had disappeared.

We'll come back to those fake statements toward the end of this story, because they're what opened the way for the fear Diana brought to her lawyer two months later.

It started there, on that August afternoon at Althorp, the road that would end on the steel table in Fulham, where the pathologist Robert Chapman would open Diana's body and write down, page by page, what he had found inside her.

Nineteen days after that afternoon at Althorp, on Tuesday, September nineteenth, nineteen ninety-five, Diana crossed London to meet the man with the statements. The meeting took place in a borrowed apartment in Knightsbridge, a few blocks from Harrods, far from the phones at Kensington Palace, which she no longer used for anything that mattered.

Charles Spencer was there, sitting in an armchair by the window, with a notepad resting on his knee. He had brought the pad to write down everything the reporter said, because he wanted to understand who this man was before trusting his sister to him.

Those handwritten pages, scribbled in a hurry in that room, would sit in a drawer for twenty-six years, until a judge read, line by line, what Martin Bashir told the princess that afternoon.

Diana arrived in dark glasses, sat on the sofa, and folded her hands in her lap. Bashir sat across from her, very close, and started talking quietly, like someone sharing a family secret.

He told her her phone was tapped. He told her her car was being followed around the city. And then he gave her names.

Patrick Jephson, Diana's private secretary, the man who had run her schedule for years, was supposedly taking money to pass along information about her. Richard Aylard, her husband's secretary, too. According to Bashir, both men were part of a network of people paid to watch the princess's every move.

Diana didn't argue. She looked at her brother for a while, and then at her own hands. It was the girl on the staircase at Park House hearing, from a stranger, that a lifetime of fear had been right all along.

Charles Spencer left that apartment with a full notepad and a bad feeling in his stomach. There were too many stories, too many names, and he started to distrust the reporter who, three weeks earlier, had won him over with a stack of papers. In the days that followed, Charles Spencer pulled back.

Diana didn't.

Bashir started calling her. He met with her again, several times, without her brother around. And inside Kensington Palace, Diana started looking at Patrick Jephson differently, measuring what she said in front of him, sliding papers into a drawer when he walked into the room.

The man she now treated as a traitor was the very one who would be sitting next to her, six weeks later, when she said out loud how she thought she was going to die.

On Monday, October thirtieth, nineteen ninety-five, Diana walked into the office of Lord Victor Mishcon, in central London. Mishcon was eighty, one of the most respected lawyers in England, and he was handling her interests in the separation. Jephson came in with her and took the chair beside her.

The conversation started with the divorce, the paperwork, the boys. Then Diana leaned over the desk and changed the subject.

She said she had received a warning from sources she considered reliable. The Queen was going to abdicate in April, and her husband would take the throne. And before then, someone was going to try to get rid of her.

Lord Mishcon picked up his pen. And Diana said the sentence he wrote down almost word for word, on a sheet of paper that would stay hidden for eight years.

An accident in my car, with the brakes tampered with, or something like that.

She went on. If they couldn't kill her, they would at least leave her so badly hurt, so shaken, that everyone would say the Princess of Wales had lost her mind.

Mishcon looked at Jephson. Jephson looked at Mishcon. Both of them told her, as gently as they could, that it made no sense, that nobody was going to tamper with the brakes on the car of the future king's mother. Diana listened in silence, thanked them, and refused to say who had told her.

The lawyer wrote it all down, signed it, dated it, and put it away. Weeks after the tunnel, Mishcon handed that sheet to the police, who kept it in a safe until two thousand three.

Before that summer with Martin Bashir, Diana had never told anyone she was afraid of dying in a car. Six weeks after the Knightsbridge apartment, she was describing the brakes.

Twenty-two months after that conversation in Mishcon's office, the car hit the pillar.

On Sunday afternoon, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven, a Royal Air Force plane landed at Northolt, outside London, with Diana's coffin draped in the royal standard. Her ex-husband, Prince Charles, and her two sisters came down the steps behind the coffin. From there, the hearse drove to the Hammersmith and Fulham mortuary.

That night, the royal coroner, John Burton, had the room opened. The man who pulled on the gloves was the pathologist Robert Chapman.

The body Chapman found on the steel table didn't look like someone who had died in a crash of that force. On the outside, almost nothing. A few small marks, a bruise here, a scrape there. The face the whole world knew was practically untouched.

The damage was on the inside.

The chest had already been opened in Paris. Chapman found the stitches that the surgeon Alain Pavie had put in during the small hours, in a small vein, about the size of a finger, right where it enters the heart. It was the left upper pulmonary vein, the one that carries blood from the lungs back into the chest.

At the moment of impact, Diana's body was thrown forward so violently that her heart shifted inside her chest, and that vein tore. A small tear. But through it, blood slowly leaked into her chest cavity, while she was still talking, still moving, still trapped in the wreckage of the Mercedes.

The doctors in Paris had sewn that tear shut with their own hands. They massaged her heart inside and out for two hours. But by the time they reached the vein, the blood she had lost wasn't coming back.

And there was one more thing about that body that Chapman noticed as soon as he started. A strong chemical smell that shouldn't have been there.

Diana's body had been embalmed in Paris, hours before her family reached the hospital. The embalming fluid had worked its way through the tissues, and some of the tests Chapman wanted to run no longer gave reliable results.

Because of that embalming, for years, Mohamed Al-Fayed repeated the same accusation to anyone who would listen. He was Dodi's father, the boyfriend who died with Diana in the tunnel. He said Diana had been pregnant with his son's child, and that the embalming had been rushed through precisely to erase that.

But there was a little of Diana's blood that no chemical had touched. On the night of the tunnel, while she bled, some of that blood ran down and pooled on the floor of the Mercedes, in the footwell, in the back seat. It was blood from before any transfusion, before the hospital, before the embalming.

That blood was collected and tested. And it had no pregnancy hormone in it.

What Diana's body showed on that table in Fulham was this: she died from a tear in the left pulmonary vein and internal bleeding, exactly as the doctors in Paris had said, and she was not pregnant, because the blood left behind in the car, untouched, showed no sign of pregnancy at all. The embalming hid no baby. Chapman wrote the cause of death in the report in one dry sentence.

A thirty-six-year-old woman, her face almost unmarked, died from a tear the size of a fingernail, while the world spent decades arguing about a pregnancy her own blood ruled out.

Except the body answers how she died, and nothing else. Two years before that steel table, Diana sat across from an eighty-year-old lawyer and described, in detail, a car accident that was going to kill her, just weeks after spending an entire afternoon hearing from Martin Bashir that everyone around her was a traitor. Who put that car inside her head, and why, is written in official documents.

On Sunday, November fifth, nineteen ninety-five, Kensington Palace was almost empty. It was Bonfire Night, the English fireworks holiday, and Diana had given the staff the night off, one by one, until the only people left in apartments eight and nine were the ones she wanted there.

Late in the afternoon, a car pulled up at the gate. Martin Bashir got out with two other men, a producer and a cameraman, carrying boxes that, from a distance, looked like new stereo equipment being delivered. Inside were the camera, the lights, and the microphones.

Patrick Jephson, the secretary who organized every minute of her life, was at home that night, and he would only learn about those boxes nine days later, when it was too late to do anything.

The three men set everything up in the upstairs sitting room. They drew the curtains, pushed the sofa into a corner, and set out two chairs, facing each other. Diana came down in a dark suit, her eyes lined in heavier black than usual, and sat in the chair on the left.

She hadn't shown the questions to anyone. Lord Mishcon didn't know. Her press secretary didn't know. Her brother, who had pulled away from Bashir after the Knightsbridge afternoon, didn't know either. The only man she trusted in that room was the same one who, two months earlier, had spread fake bank statements across a table at Althorp.

Outside, in the London sky, the first fireworks started going off. The cameraman pressed the button.

Bashir started with the marriage. Then he asked about food, and Diana told him, looking straight at him, that in her first years at the palace she would stuff herself and throw up four, five times a day. That she did it because she felt too small, too ugly, worthless.

He asked whether she had ever hurt herself on purpose. Diana took a deep breath and answered without looking away.

I hurt my arms and my legs.

Almost two years later, Robert Chapman would examine that same body on the steel table in Fulham, inch by inch, and note every mark in the twenty-two pages of his report.

And it was on one of those pages, page nine, that he left, in his own handwriting, the line someone would later cover in black ink.

In the sitting room at Kensington, the conversation moved onto the ground Bashir had spent two months preparing. He asked whether there were people in the palace working against her. Diana said yes. She said the enemy was her husband's circle, that the people around her leaked everything, that her phone wasn't safe.

They were, almost word for word, the things she had heard in the Knightsbridge apartment. Now they were on tape, in her voice, for the whole world to hear.

Bashir asked whether she thought they would manage to push her out of the way. Diana smiled faintly and said she wasn't going to go quietly, that she would fight to the end.

Then he asked whether Camilla Parker Bowles had been a factor in the end of the marriage, and Diana lowered her eyes, raised them again, and said the sentence that within hours would travel around the world.

Well, there were three of us in this marriage. So it was a bit crowded.

The recording went on for hours. By the time the three men took down the lights and carried the boxes back to the car, the fireworks outside were over. Diana climbed the stairs to her bedroom alone, in a palace with no staff, certain she had done the right thing.

On Tuesday, November fourteenth, Diana called Patrick Jephson into her office. It was her husband's forty-seventh birthday. She told him, almost in passing, that she had given an interview to Panorama and that it would air the following Monday.

Jephson stood there, frozen, not knowing what to say. The man who had sat beside her in Mishcon's office, listening to her fear about the brakes, found out that morning that he had been left out of the most serious decision of her life. And he understood, from the way she looked at him, that she no longer trusted him.

He didn't know why. He didn't yet know that Bashir, in a room in Knightsbridge, had told the princess that her own secretary was being paid to spy on her.

Monday, November twentieth, nineteen ninety-five, was Queen Elizabeth and Prince Philip's forty-eighth wedding anniversary. The BBC schedule had Panorama at nine forty that night.

All over England, families ate dinner early. The pubs turned on their televisions. According to the next day's ratings, almost twenty-three million people watched, in the United Kingdom alone.

Diana watched at Kensington. Her sons were far away. Harry, eleven, was at boarding school at Ludgrove. William, thirteen, was at Eton, in his first year there.

That night, William sat in his housemaster's study at Eton and watched his mother on the screen, eyes lined in black, telling millions of strangers about the vomiting, the cut arms, and his father's mistress. He watched to the end without saying a word.

When Diana called afterward, the boy picked up, heard his mother's voice, and hung up.

Right after the program ended, Nicholas Soames, a government minister and a childhood friend of Prince Charles, appeared live on Newsnight, also on the BBC. He said, on camera, that the princess showed signs of being in the advanced stages of paranoia.

It was exactly what Diana had told Mishcon, three weeks earlier, that they would do to her. Call her crazy.

From that night on, every fear Bashir had watered seemed to come true. Her husband's friends attacked her on television. The next day's newspapers split the country down the middle. And inside the palace, the people still on her side started to leave.

Patrick Jephson wrote his resignation letter in December. He had spent eight years at Diana's side, on the trips, in the hospitals, on the nights she cried in the car, and he walked out the door at Kensington without her asking him to stay. Her press secretary, Geoff Crawford, asked to leave too.

The week before Christmas, a messenger from Buckingham Palace crossed London with an envelope. Inside was a letter handwritten by the Queen. She sent identical copies to her son and her daughter-in-law, asking them both for a quick divorce, for the good of the country and the boys.

Diana read the letter sitting on her bed, alone.

Her brother was at Althorp, suspicious of the reporter he had introduced her to. Her secretary was gone. Her mother-in-law wanted a divorce. Her older son had hung up on her. In six weeks, the woman the whole world had watched speak on television was left with no one to trust inside her own home.

Only one phone kept ringing, and it was Martin Bashir's.

The fear Bashir planted in that woman, and the exact reason he planted it, is written in papers the BBC kept in a drawer for twenty-five years, until a judge opened that drawer.

Martin Bashir's phone kept ringing through nineteen ninety-six. Diana no longer used the landlines at Kensington for anything. She called him from her cell phone, almost always at night, sitting on the floor of her bedroom with her back against the bed, and repeated what she had been feeling: that someone was reading her mail, that someone was following her car.

On the other end, the reporter listened and agreed. Every time she hung up, she was more alone than before she called.

And every one of those late-night conversations stripped one more piece of protection from her path, until all that was left, on the night in Paris, was a single man in the front seat of the Mercedes.

On Wednesday, February twenty-eighth, nineteen ninety-six, Diana crossed the gardens to St James's Palace to meet with her husband. The aides waiting outside counted forty-five minutes of conversation behind a closed door. When she came out, she had her office tell the newspapers she was agreeing to the divorce, before Buckingham Palace was ready to say anything.

The Queen found out from the press. That same afternoon, Prince Charles's aides started describing the princess, behind the scenes, as a woman out of control, unpredictable, who wouldn't listen to anyone.

It was the word she had predicted in Mishcon's office. Now she was hearing it from every direction, and she no longer had Patrick Jephson to tell her she was exaggerating.

The lawyers spent the spring fighting over numbers. In July, the settlement was done: about seventeen million pounds, the apartment at Kensington, and the right to keep seeing her sons as before. In exchange, Diana lost the title of Her Royal Highness. She was still Princess of Wales in name, but as far as the Crown was concerned, she was no longer part of the family.

Also in July, Diana wrote to more than a hundred charities to say she was stepping down as their patron. She kept only six: the homeless, AIDS patients, cancer patients, leprosy patients, Great Ormond Street children's hospital, and the English National Ballet.

On Wednesday morning, August twenty-eighth, nineteen ninety-six, in a family court office at Somerset House, on the banks of the Thames, a clerk stamped the final decree. Fifteen years of marriage ended with a stamp and a signature, with neither of them in the room.

Diana spent that morning in her office at Kensington. One of the secretaries said later that, at noon, someone came in with boxes of new stationery. The crest was still there. The letters of Her Royal Highness had disappeared from the letterhead.

She picked up a sheet, looked at it for a while, and put it in a drawer without writing anything.

What hurt most that week came from a fourteen-year-old boy. William went into his mother's room, sat on the edge of the bed, and said a sentence Diana repeated to her friends until her last summer.

I don't care what they call you. To me, you're Mummy.

She cried in front of her son, something she had been avoiding for months.

There was still one last thing standing between Diana and the world: the officers of Scotland Yard. Since nineteen ninety-three, she had been dismissing her bodyguards, one at a time, convinced they were reporting to the palace where she went and who she talked to. After the divorce, the police offered to keep officers with her at official engagements. Diana said no.

One evening in September, an officer from royal protection came up to the office at Kensington with a form. He explained carefully what she was turning down. Diana signed without reading to the end, handed back the pen, and thanked him. A year earlier, she had heard from Bashir that even her own secretary was being paid to watch her.

That signature cost dearly. The men trained by the police to protect the mother of the future king were on the other side of the English Channel the night the Mercedes entered the tunnel.

Diana started driving herself around the city. A friend of hers, Simone Simmons, said that that fall, the princess took her car to a garage outside London, far from the palace mechanics. She asked them to check the brakes, part by part. The mechanic found nothing. She paid in cash and asked him to check again.

It was the warning that had reached Mishcon's office, now guiding her hands on the steering wheel.

And it's that same woman, who that fall was afraid to get into a car, that Robert Chapman would examine on the steel table in Fulham. On page nine, he wrote down what he found on her.

In January nineteen ninety-seven, Diana went to Angola with the Red Cross. On Wednesday, January fifteenth, in Huambo, she put on a blast-proof vest and a plastic visor. She walked slowly along a strip of red earth, cleared through the middle of a minefield, in front of dozens of photographers.

There, without a single one of the Queen's police officers nearby, she finally seemed at peace. A minister in the Conservative government called the princess a loose cannon. She kept walking.

That spring, Diana was in love with a heart surgeon, Hasnat Khan, from Pakistan, who worked at the Royal Brompton Hospital. She talked about marrying him, about living with him far from England. But Khan couldn't stand the photographers, and at the start of the summer the two drifted apart.

On Tuesday, July first, nineteen ninety-seven, Diana turned thirty-six. That night, she went to the Tate Gallery's hundredth anniversary party in London, in a short black dress, and came home early to Kensington. It was the last birthday she would ever have.

That same week, an invitation arrived from Mohamed Al-Fayed, the owner of Harrods, who had known the Spencer family for years. He was offering his house in Saint-Tropez, in the south of France, for her to spend a few days with the boys. There she would have something Diana no longer had in London: security, armed men, high gates.

Diana accepted. On Friday, July eleventh, she boarded Al-Fayed's private jet with William and Harry. On Monday, July fourteenth, his eldest son, Dodi, arrived from Paris.

Dodi was forty-two and a film producer. He was kind, laughed easily, and sent flowers every day. Diana spent her afternoons with him on the yacht Jonikal, while the boys dove off the deck.

Around them, the men now protecting the princess were Al-Fayed's team, paid by him. One of them was a twenty-nine-year-old former Welsh soldier named Trevor Rees-Jones.

Six weeks later, Trevor Rees-Jones would be sitting in the passenger seat of the Mercedes, and he would be the only one to come out of the tunnel alive.

The summer went by fast. On Tuesday, July twenty-second, Diana went to Milan for the funeral of the designer Gianni Versace, murdered on his own doorstep, and hugged the singer Elton John, who was crying in a pew of the cathedral. She went back to Dodi's yacht, then to Paris, then to London. In August, she spent three days in Bosnia, listening to widows and boys who had lost their legs to mines.

In early August, an Italian photographer, hidden in a boat off the coast of Corsica, took the picture the whole world knows: Diana and Dodi embracing on deck, in swimsuits. The British newspapers paid fortunes for it. From that day on, every port where the Jonikal stopped woke up surrounded by speedboats full of lenses.

Diana was happy and afraid at the same time. She told a friend, over the phone, that she felt surrounded, that she couldn't go to the beach anymore without seeing a boat following her.

On Saturday, August thirtieth, nineteen ninety-seven, Al-Fayed's jet took off from the island of Sardinia and landed that afternoon at Le Bourget airport, outside Paris. Diana came down the steps in dark glasses. Down below, the photographers' motorcycles were already starting their engines.

She got into the back seat of a black car. Beside her, Dodi. In front, Trevor Rees-Jones. No officer from Scotland Yard, because she herself had signed that form at Kensington.

Diana drove into Paris with the fear of the brakes still in her head. That fear wasn't hers alone. Someone had put it there, for a reason, and that someone was the first to profit from every person she pushed away.

At ten o'clock on the night of Saturday, August thirtieth, Diana and Dodi got up from their table in the restaurant of the Hotel Ritz, in Paris, after ten minutes. People were staring too much. The two of them went upstairs and had dinner in the suite. Out front, on the Place Vendôme, more than thirty photographers waited with their motorcycles running.

Someone on the security team had the idea of leaving through the back. The camera on the rue Cambon door recorded the exit: twelve twenty a.m., Sunday, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven.

Diana crossed the sidewalk with her head down and got into the back seat of a black Mercedes. Dodi sat to her left. In front, Trevor Rees-Jones. At the wheel, Henri Paul, forty-one, the deputy head of security at the Ritz.

Neither of the two in the back seat buckled a seat belt.

That unbuckled belt would leave on her body the marks Robert Chapman copied down, one by one, across the twenty-two pages, and one of them had nothing to do with the belt.

The Mercedes went down through the Place de la Concorde and took the expressway that runs along the Seine. Behind it, the photographers' motorcycles stuck to the bumper. According to the French experts, Henri Paul entered the Pont de l'Alma tunnel at more than sixty miles an hour, where the limit was thirty.

By the investigation's calculations, it was twelve twenty-three when the car lost control on the curve at the entrance. The Mercedes slammed head-on into the thirteenth concrete pillar of the tunnel, spun, and came to rest sideways, facing the way it had come.

For minutes, the horn blared without stopping. Henri Paul had fallen dead over the steering wheel.

Dodi was dead in the back seat. Trevor Rees-Jones, in the front, had his face crushed against the dashboard, but he was breathing. And Diana was on the floor of the car, almost on her knees, in the gap between the seats, her head down and her hair over her face.

She was alive. Blood was trickling slowly onto the carpet, in the footwell, and it was that blood, collected right there, that years later would show she wasn't pregnant.

The flashes started before the ambulance. A doctor driving through the tunnel, Frédéric Mailliez, stopped, held her head, and called for help on his cell phone. Shortly after, fire department sergeant Xavier Gourmelon arrived, knelt beside the door, and took Diana's hand.

She looked at him and said one sentence, in English, which Gourmelon repeated before the London jury eleven years later.

My God. What's happened?

When the firefighters managed to pull her out of the wreckage and lay her on the stretcher, her heart stopped. Gourmelon pressed on her chest with both hands, counting out loud, until her heart started beating again. He thought she was going to make it.

The ambulance left the tunnel more than an hour after the crash. It moved slowly, on the doctors' orders, because any jolt could kill her. Near the Jardin des Plantes, Diana's blood pressure dropped so low that the driver stopped in the middle of the street and the doctors worked with the vehicle standing still.

The emergency log read two oh six a.m. when the stretcher came through the doors of the Pitié-Salpêtrière hospital.

According to the surgeon Monsef Dahman, who was on duty, at two fifteen Diana's heart stopped again. There was no time to get her to an operating room. Dahman opened her chest right there, on the emergency room gurney.

The surgeon Alain Pavie came running from home. He pushed the ribs apart, felt around with his fingers in the blood, and found the tear in the left upper pulmonary vein, right where it meets the heart. He stitched it.

They gave her shocks. They gave her adrenaline. They squeezed her heart with their own hands, inside and out, for two hours.

At four in the morning, the doctors stopped. Diana was thirty-six. At six, the anesthesiologist Bruno Riou walked into a room full of reporters and announced the death of the Princess of Wales.

Dahman's incisions, Pavie's stitches, the marks from the shocks, all of it stayed on that body, and it was among them that Chapman found the mark that made him pick up his blue pen and write in the margin of page nine.

At Balmoral Castle, in Scotland, the boys' father woke them in the morning. Harry, twelve, wrote in his memoir that his father sat on the edge of his bed and said: Darling boy, Mummy's been in a car crash. And then: They tried, darling boy. She didn't make it.

Harry didn't cry. It was only years later that he was able to cry for his mother.

In Paris, Sunday dawned hot. Diana was moved to a room on the first floor of the hospital, with windows facing the sun. The nurses said later that by early afternoon the room was already past eighty-six degrees, and that they put dry ice under the sheet.

No Spencer had reached Paris yet. Her ex-husband was still in Scotland, with the boys.

It was during those hours that an embalmer from a French funeral home, Jean Monceau, walked into the room with a metal case. The door stayed closed for close to two hours. When he came out, Diana's blood had been replaced with a chemical fluid, and her body was no longer the same one that had come in on the stretcher.

French law requires the family's authorization to embalm someone. The family wasn't there. The official investigation said the decision was made because of the heat, ahead of the relatives' visit.

Twenty-nine years later, the name of whoever gave that order is still missing from every public document in the case.

The woman who spent two years afraid of people working against her behind her back lay there, in a stifling room, while a man she had never seen worked on her body on the orders of someone nobody has named to this day.

Around five in the afternoon, Prince Charles walked into the hospital with Diana's two sisters, Sarah and Jane. By the time they reached the bed, the work of the metal case was already done.

That same night, the body was in Fulham, and the smell of the fluid was the first thing Chapman wrote down.

In London, the coffin lay in the royal chapel at St James's Palace. Outside Kensington Palace, people left flowers until they covered the entire lawn, and the scent of the flowers reached the windows of the apartment where she had signed the police form.

On Saturday, September sixth, nineteen ninety-seven, the coffin left on a horse-drawn gun carriage. On top of it, white roses and an envelope with a single word, handwritten by Harry: Mummy.

Behind the coffin, on foot, walked five men. The boys' father, their grandfather Philip, their uncle Charles Spencer, and the two boys themselves, William and Harry, fifteen and twelve, in dark suits, looking at the ground.

At Westminster Abbey, Charles Spencer stepped up to the pulpit. He was the brother who, two years earlier, had believed the fake statements at Althorp. He said that his sister, who bore the name of the ancient goddess of hunting, had ended up the most hunted person of the modern age.

Outside, the crowd listening over the loudspeakers began to applaud, and the applause rolled in through the doors of the abbey until it reached the pews.

Afterward, the hearse drove up the highway to Althorp. Diana was buried on an island in the middle of the lake, on the land where she had spent her teenage years dreaming of marrying for love.

Twelve days later, on Thursday, September eighteenth, Lord Victor Mishcon, eighty-two, walked into Scotland Yard headquarters with a folder under his arm. He sat down across from the commissioner of London's police, Sir Paul Condon, opened the folder, and handed over the sheet from October thirtieth, nineteen ninety-five.

Condon read the princess's words about the brakes on her car and had the sheet locked in the safe.

That note held Diana's fear, word for word. The name of the man who had fed that fear was somewhere else: in a folder inside the BBC itself, with the Althorp statements inside it.

On Monday, April seventh, two thousand eight, eleven jurors filed into Court Seventy-Three of the High Court in London. They had spent six months hearing more than two hundred and fifty witnesses. In the front row of the public gallery, in a dark suit, sat Mohamed Al-Fayed, his chin resting on his hands.

The foreman of the jury stood and read the verdict before the judge, Lord Justice Scott Baker. Unlawful killing. Diana and Dodi had died because of the grossly negligent driving of Henri Paul and of the vehicles chasing the Mercedes. And the lack of seat belts in the back seat had contributed to both deaths.

It was the phrase English law uses to say it wasn't an accident. Nine jurors voted in favor, two against.

On the judge's desk, among the evidence folders, sat the copy of Robert Chapman's report. On page nine, the black band ran across the margin.

The jurors decided how Diana died without reading what was under that ink, and what was there said less about the tunnel than about the two years that came before it.

Twelve years later, Matt Wiessler, the graphic designer who had mocked up the fake statements on his computer, decided to talk. He had been pushed out of the BBC in nineteen ninety-six, and he spent almost twenty-five years being treated as the guilty party in a story that wasn't his.

In November twenty twenty, Charles Spencer opened a drawer at Althorp and took out the notepad from that afternoon in Knightsbridge. The pages were yellowed, his handwriting hurried, slanting to the right. He handed everything to the BBC, and the BBC brought in a retired Supreme Court justice, Lord Dyson, to investigate.

On Thursday, May twentieth, twenty twenty-one, Lord Dyson's report was published. More than a hundred pages. And inside it, copied line by line, was what Martin Bashir told the princess on the sofa of that borrowed apartment, on Tuesday, September nineteenth, nineteen ninety-five.

Her phone was tapped. Her car was being followed. Patrick Jephson and Richard Aylard were taking money to watch her. And in the middle of the page, underlined by Charles Spencer's pen, there was one more thing the reporter said that afternoon.

The Queen had heart trouble. She was going to leave the throne. Diana's husband was going to be king.

Six weeks later, in Lord Mishcon's office, Diana leaned over the desk and repeated that same story. The Queen was going to abdicate in April. Her husband would take over. And before then, someone was going to tamper with the brakes on her car.

It was the same story, on both pieces of paper. The voice was Diana's, but the story had come out of Martin Bashir's mouth.

The Queen didn't have heart trouble. Jephson never took a penny from anyone to spy on the princess. The Althorp statements were a fabrication. Lord Dyson wrote that Bashir acted deceitfully, that he faked documents to get to Diana, and that the BBC itself, in an internal investigation in nineteen ninety-six, covered it all up and let the reporter carry on.

He did it to get an interview. To have, in a sitting room at Kensington, on a night of fireworks, the most famous woman in the world sitting in the chair on the left, afraid of everyone around her, except him.

Diana got into that car in Paris carrying a fear a journalist had invented to win ratings.

That same night in May, William sat down in front of a camera, in a blue suit, hands clasped, and read a statement looking straight into the lens. He was the boy from Eton who had hung up on his mother, now thirty-eight with three children of his own.

He said the reporter had made lurid and false claims about the royal family, which played on her fears and fueled paranoia. And he said it brought him indescribable sadness to know that the BBC's failures had contributed significantly to the fear, paranoia, and isolation he remembered from those final years with his mother.

Then William paused and said the sentence that weighed most.

If the BBC had properly investigated the complaints raised in nineteen ninety-five, my mother would have known that she had been deceived.

She died without knowing. She died believing Patrick Jephson was betraying her, that the officers of Scotland Yard were reporting her movements to the palace, that someone was tampering with her brakes. She died without knowing that the one man she trusted that fall was the only one lying to her.

This October, twenty twenty-six, when the envelope with no return address arrived at Scotland Yard, investigators found, clipped behind page twenty-two, two sheets of notebook paper written in the shaky hand of a sick man. It was the man from the Fulham morgue.

He wrote that on the night of Sunday, August thirty-first, nineteen ninety-seven, Robert Chapman finished the examination close to midnight, took off his gloves, left the typed pages with his notes on them, and went home. It fell to the man on the night shift to take the report to the records room.

He sat alone, under a cold light, turning the pages. The torn vein. The stitches from Paris. The smell of the embalming. Until he reached page nine, where Chapman had described the marks on the skin, one by one, and where the blue pen had left the typed text and run down into the margin.

It was four short lines, which the man copied out twenty-nine years later, from memory, without getting a word wrong, because he spent the rest of his life unable to forget them.

Fine, recent cuts on the inside of the left forearm, a few weeks old. Self-inflicted. The same pattern she described on the BBC. This woman was living in fear.

That was what the special light in the lab had brought out in pieces. Beneath the marks from the shocks, Dahman's incisions, and Pavie's stitches, Diana's arm held cuts she had made herself that summer. The summer of the yacht, of Dodi, and of flowers every day. The summer the whole world thought she was finally happy.

She was still hurting herself. Two years after that sitting room at Kensington, the girl from the staircase at Park House was still cutting herself in secret.

On Tuesday, September ninth, three days after the burial at Althorp, the man from the morgue opened the door of the records room and found an older man in a gray suit, with the badge of the royal coroner's office clipped to his jacket pocket. The report was open to page nine. In his hand was a thick black marker.

The man in the suit ran the marker across the margin once, then again, pressing so hard the tip tore through the paper. When he noticed someone in the doorway, he didn't look up. He only said: this doesn't help anyone. The boys will read this one day.

They crossed out the very line that told what had been done to that woman before the tunnel. That way, the court would talk about speed, alcohol, motorcycles, and seat belts. The bill would go to the driver and the photographers, and nobody would ask who had filled Diana with fear for two years.

The man from the morgue went home that morning afraid. A week later, he carried the twenty-two original pages out under his coat and locked them in a drawer, where the pages stayed until his daughter put the envelope in the mail, this month.

The BBC promised never to air that interview again. Martin Bashir left the network a few days before the report came out. And in a Scotland Yard archive, inside a folder, the sheet Lord Mishcon dated Monday, October thirtieth, nineteen ninety-five, is still there.

The old lawyer's handwriting is still on it today, a little faded. An accident in my car, with the brakes tampered with, or something like that.

Diana spent two years afraid of the wrong car, and the real danger was a man she let in because he said he was looking out for her. The fear she brought to her lawyer was the same fear left marked on her arm, in a line someone crossed out so no one would read it.

If someone is making you afraid of the very people who love you, call one of those people today and tell them everything.
