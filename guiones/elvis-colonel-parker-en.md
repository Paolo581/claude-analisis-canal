# ELVIS PRESLEY: The DISGUSTING Secret of the Man Who ROBBED Him for 20 Years

English version of the Portuguese script. Numbers are spelled out for narration. Paragraph breaks mark the pauses.

Elvis Presley was the most famous man on the planet, and he never played a single concert in Europe. The reason was hidden in the past of the man who controlled his every move.

A dirty secret, kept for decades, that cost the King his freedom, and even half of the money he earned.

Stay until the end, because you're going to find out who this man really was. And why nobody around Elvis had the courage to stop him.

But to understand how someone managed to take control of the biggest star in the world, you have to go back to a two-room house in Mississippi. Because the wound this man exploited for twenty years was opened right there, long before Elvis was famous.

January eighth, nineteen thirty-five.

Tupelo, Mississippi.

In a wooden house so small you could see from one door straight through to the other, a woman named Gladys Presley went into labor.

The house had been built by her own husband, Vernon, with borrowed money. Two rooms and a plank floor.

In the early hours of that morning, Gladys gave birth to two boys.

The first was stillborn. He was given the name Jesse Garon.

Thirty-five minutes later, the second was born. Alive. He was given the name Elvis Aaron.

Jesse was buried in an unmarked grave, in a cemetery in town. And Elvis grew up hearing his mother talk about the brother who didn't survive.

Remember this detail. Because the boy who was born after a dead brother grew up feeling he was carrying two lives on his back. And that weight would follow Elvis to his very last day.

The Presleys were truly poor.

Vernon took whatever work came along. Gladys worked as a seamstress in a factory. Many times, there wasn't money for the basics.

When Elvis was three, the floor fell out from under the family.

In nineteen thirty-eight, Vernon Presley was arrested.

He had altered the amount on a check he received for selling a hog. A small crime, committed by a desperate man. But Mississippi justice showed no mercy.

Vernon was sentenced to three years at Parchman Farm, one of the harshest prisons in the American South.

He served about eight months.

Meanwhile, Gladys and little Elvis lost the two-room house. They had to live off the charity of relatives.

Picture a three-year-old boy, no father at home, watching his mother cry with worry every single night.

That's where Elvis learned two things he would carry forever.

The first: his mother was the center of the universe. Everything he did in life would be, in some way, to protect Gladys.

The second: a strong man, a man who took care of things, was something that house was missing.

This is where everything changes. Because twenty years later, someone was going to offer to fill that empty place. With a smile, a cigar, and a contract in his hand.

Vernon came home from prison. But life stayed hard.

Gladys overprotected her son in a way that everyone noticed. She walked Elvis to school even when he was far too old for it. She wouldn't let the boy swim in the river with the other kids, terrified of losing the only child she had left.

On his eleventh birthday, in January of nineteen forty-six, Gladys took Elvis to a hardware store in Tupelo to buy him a present.

Elvis wanted a rifle. His mother said no.

He walked out of that store with a guitar.

A cheap guitar, bought with the family's carefully counted money. Nobody in that store imagined they were selling the instrument that would change the music of the twentieth century.

In nineteen forty-eight, the Presleys moved to Memphis, Tennessee, chasing a better life.

They lived in public housing for poor families. Elvis went to Humes High School, where he was shy and seen as a weirdo by his classmates, with his hair slicked back with grease and the unusual clothes he bought on Beale Street, the heart of Black music in Memphis.

In nineteen fifty-three, shortly after graduating, he walked into a small studio in Memphis, in the same building as the Sun Records label.

He paid a few dollars to record two songs on an acetate disc. According to the most often told version of this story, it was a gift for his mother.

Not long after, he got a job driving a truck for an electric company.

Notice this. The first record of Elvis Presley's life was recorded with Gladys in mind. And losing Gladys, years later, would be the blow that left Elvis in the hands of a single man.

The owner of Sun Records, Sam Phillips, paid attention to that young man.

In July of nineteen fifty-four, he called Elvis in to record for real. During a break, Elvis started fooling around with a blues song by Arthur Crudup, "That's All Right," singing it fast and laughing.

Sam Phillips told everyone to stop and record exactly that.

Days later, the song played on a Memphis radio station. The station's phone never stopped ringing. The DJ had to play the record again, and again, and again.

A nineteen-year-old truck driver had just changed the course of music.

Within a few months, Elvis was singing all across the American South. The girls screamed. The parents were scandalized by the way he moved his hips.

The success started attracting people of every kind.

Among them was a heavyset man, a cigar in his mouth and a strange accent, who introduced himself as the Colonel.

The first object in this story is a contract. A few sheets of paper that Elvis would sign so young that, under the law at the time, his parents had to sign too. Sheets of paper that would decide who ran his life for the next twenty years. What was written on them looked normal. Until you find out who the man signing on the other side really was.

The name he used was Tom Parker.

Nobody really knew where he had come from. He claimed to have been born in West Virginia. He had worked in traveling carnivals, selling sideshow attractions, and later as a manager for country singers.

The title of Colonel was purely honorary, a favor handed out by a governor of Louisiana to a man who knew how to make important friends. Tom Parker never commanded a single soldier.

But Tom Parker had one talent nobody could deny. He understood money like very few people did. And when he saw that kid from Memphis shaking his hips on a stage, he understood instantly what he had in his hands.

In August of nineteen fifty-five, Elvis signed his first contract with the Colonel.

Elvis was twenty. Under the law, he was still a minor. Vernon and Gladys signed alongside him.

Gladys didn't like that man. She felt there was something wrong with him. But the Colonel promised fame, money, and protection for her son. And the family that had lived off relatives' charity, that had watched the father get taken away to prison, was in no position to refuse.

Hold on to Gladys's suspicion. Because Elvis's mother was one of the very few people who looked at the Colonel and sensed there was something hidden. And when she left the scene, there was nobody left to say that to her son.

The Colonel moved fast.

In November of nineteen fifty-five, he negotiated Elvis's move from tiny Sun Records to the giant RCA. The price of the transfer was forty thousand dollars, a fortune at the time, the highest amount ever paid for a young singer up to that point.

In March of nineteen fifty-six, the Colonel officially became Elvis Presley's exclusive manager. His commission was twenty-five percent.

And then came the explosion.

"Heartbreak Hotel" went to the top of the charts. Elvis appeared on the biggest television shows in the United States. In September of nineteen fifty-six, when he performed on The Ed Sullivan Show, around sixty million people were sitting in front of their televisions.

Not all of it was glory. In July of nineteen fifty-six, on a television show hosted by Steve Allen, Elvis was put in a tuxedo and made to sing "Hound Dog" to an actual dog, sitting on a stool. The audience laughed. Years later, Elvis would say that was one of the most humiliating performances of his life. But it was ratings, and the Colonel wanted ratings.

On his last appearance on The Ed Sullivan Show, in January of nineteen fifty-seven, the cameras filmed Elvis only from the waist up. The hip-shaking was considered too indecent for American families.

In little more than a year, the truck driver from Memphis had become the most famous man in the country.

The Colonel was everywhere.

He decided the contracts and the shows, even the interviews. He closed deals to sell anything that had Elvis's face on it. From lipstick to dolls. He even sold buttons that said "I Hate Elvis," so he could make money off the people who didn't like the singer too.

Do the math. Twenty-five percent of everything Elvis earned went straight into the Colonel's pocket. And that was only the beginning. Years later, that percentage would double in several contracts. And Elvis would sign.

In March of nineteen fifty-seven, Elvis bought a mansion in Memphis.

Graceland.

He paid one hundred and two thousand, five hundred dollars. And he bought it, above all, for Gladys.

The woman who had lost the two-room house in Tupelo now lived in a mansion with white columns. Elvis had given his mother everything that had been missing from his childhood.

But his mother didn't seem happy.

Gladys missed the simple life. She missed her son, who was almost never home. And she began to get sick.

At the end of nineteen fifty-seven, a letter arrived that would change everything.

Elvis had been drafted by the Army.

Many expected him to go into Special Services, the entertainment branch, singing for the troops, with privileges and far from the heavy work. The Army actually offered that.

But the Colonel had other plans.

He preferred that Elvis serve as an ordinary soldier. That way, the singer wouldn't do a single free show for the government, and the image of the rebellious kid would become that of a disciplined patriot.

In March of nineteen fifty-eight, Elvis entered the barracks and had his famous pompadour shaved off in front of the photographers. After training, he would be sent to Germany, where he would serve for almost two years.

The second object in this story is a passport.

Or rather, the lack of one.

During the almost two years Elvis served in Germany, the man who controlled every step of his career never once crossed the ocean to visit him. Not a single time.

Remember that. The Colonel tracked every cent Elvis earned. But for two years, he never set foot in Europe. And the reason why is the secret that will explain everything that happened to Elvis Presley for the rest of his life.

Even before Elvis shipped out, the blow came that he never got over.

In August of nineteen fifty-eight, Gladys Presley was hospitalized in Memphis. She was very sick, with liver and heart problems.

Elvis got emergency leave and rushed to the hospital.

On August fourteenth, nineteen fifty-eight, Gladys died. She was forty-six years old.

People who were close to Elvis in those days said he looked like a lost child. He stayed beside his mother's body, talking to her, crying without stopping.

At the funeral, he had to be held up to stay on his feet.

The woman who had held the family together when her husband went to prison, and who had distrusted the Colonel from day one, was no longer there.

Imagine for a moment that it was you. Twenty-three years old, one of the most famous men in the world, and the only person you truly trusted has just died. Now imagine who was standing right beside you, ready to decide everything on your behalf.

Weeks later, Elvis crossed the ocean by ship, bound for Germany.

He took his father, Vernon, and his grandmother with him. He rented a house in the town of Bad Nauheim, off the base.

It was there, in September of nineteen fifty-nine, that he met an American girl, the stepdaughter of an Air Force officer.

Her name was Priscilla Beaulieu.

She was fourteen.

Elvis was twenty-four.

Priscilla would become the woman of Elvis's life. And years later, one of the people who would take the Colonel to court.

In March of nineteen sixty, Elvis finished his military service and went back to the United States.

Europe would never see him up close again. Those two years in uniform were the only time the biggest star in the world ever spent on that continent. And he didn't play a single show there.

And when Elvis stepped back onto American soil, the Colonel already had a plan ready for the next ten years. A plan that would make millions for both of them. And that would turn the King of Rock and Roll into a man trapped inside a movie factory he himself came to hate.

The Colonel's plan had a simple name. Hollywood.

Between nineteen sixty and nineteen sixty-nine, Elvis Presley made more than twenty-five movies.

Almost all of them with the same formula. A pretty location, a light romance, and Elvis singing a new song every fifteen minutes.

The movies were cheap to make and guaranteed a profit. And every movie came with a soundtrack album that sold on its own.

By the mid-sixties, Elvis was getting around a million dollars per movie, plus a share of the profits. He was one of the highest-paid actors in Hollywood.

From every one of those contracts, a generous slice went to the Colonel.

For the Colonel, it was the perfect machine. And Elvis, little by little, started to feel trapped inside it.

He wanted to be a real actor. He admired James Dean and Marlon Brando. He dreamed of serious, dramatic roles that would show he was more than a pretty face shaking his hips on screen.

According to people who worked with him, offers for more ambitious films did come along. But the Colonel wanted a big paycheck and a guaranteed soundtrack. And the serious roles kept falling by the wayside.

In conversations with friends, Elvis trashed his own movies. He was ashamed of many of the songs he was forced to record.

But he kept signing.

And this is where the first object in this story comes back. That contract from nineteen fifty-five. Because in early nineteen sixty-seven, it was going to be rewritten. And the new version would make the Colonel a partner in half of Elvis's life.

January, nineteen sixty-seven.

Elvis signed a new agreement with the Colonel.

Under the document, across a series of deals, the profits would now be split down the middle. Fifty percent for Elvis. Fifty percent for the manager.

Almost no artist manager charged anything like that. The industry standard was somewhere between ten and twenty-five percent.

The artist, who sweated on stage and on film sets, would now split everything equally with the man who handled the paperwork.

Elvis signed.

Since the age of twenty, he had gotten used to letting the Colonel decide everything.

A few months later, the Colonel even decided his wedding.

May first, nineteen sixty-seven. Las Vegas.

Priscilla had moved to Memphis in nineteen sixty-three, at seventeen, with her parents' permission, to finish high school at a Catholic school in the city and live at Graceland. And now, finally, she and Elvis were getting married.

The ceremony took place in a small room at the Aladdin Hotel and lasted just a few minutes.

The Colonel handled everything, from the guest list to the press conference right afterward.

Several of Elvis's closest friends, guys who had grown up with him in Memphis and were with him every single day, found out they would not be allowed into the room for the ceremony. Some of them were hurt for the rest of their lives.

Stop on this scene for a second. The King of Rock and Roll getting married in a hotel room, surrounded by photographers, with his best friends outside the door. And the man who decided all of it wasn't even family.

Nine months later, on February first, nineteen sixty-eight, Lisa Marie Presley was born.

Elvis's only child.

At the end of that same year, Elvis did something the Colonel hadn't planned.

The Colonel wanted a Christmas special on television. Elvis in a suit, singing Christmas carols, everything nice and tame.

But the show's director, a young man named Steve Binder, had a different idea. And Elvis, for the first time in a very long time, sided with someone who wasn't the Colonel.

In December of nineteen sixty-eight, America turned on the television and saw an Elvis who seemed to have been reborn. Dressed in black leather, drenched in sweat, singing again the way he had in the fifties.

That special became known as the Comeback Special. The return of the King.

The question a lot of people asked afterward, looking back, is the most painful one of all. How much talent did the Colonel waste in those ten years of movies?

But Elvis's rebirth brought a problem the Colonel couldn't solve. Because from that moment on, the whole world wanted to see the King live. And the whole world, for the Colonel, was forbidden territory.

The invitations started pouring in from every direction.

From England to Japan, from Australia to South America.

Concert promoters offered fortunes to get Elvis Presley on a stage. European fans, who had been waiting for more than a decade, were ready to pack stadiums.

The Colonel said no. One offer after another.

Now hold on to this fact, because it is the key to everything. Apart from three shows in Canada, in nineteen fifty-seven, Elvis Presley never sang outside the United States. Not once, in his entire career. And the reason had nothing to do with Elvis.

The excuses kept changing. Sometimes it was security, sometimes the lack of a stadium big enough.

Meanwhile, Elvis was placed somewhere the Colonel felt right at home.

Las Vegas.

Starting in July of nineteen sixty-nine, Elvis began a residency at the International Hotel, which later became the Hilton. Sold-out shows, twice a night, week after week. A residency that would repeat itself, year after year, until close to the end of his life.

While Elvis sang on stage, the Colonel spent hours at the roulette table in the casino, in the same hotel. Hold on to that image, because it's going to come back with numbers you won't forget.

Around this same time, something else started growing in Elvis's life, far from the public eye.

The pills.

Elvis had first encountered amphetamines back in the Army, in Germany, where they circulated among soldiers to get through long shifts. Over the years came pills to sleep, and pills to get through two shows a night.

Everything came through doctors' prescriptions. In Elvis's mind, medicine prescribed by a doctor was medicine. Drugs were something you got on the street.

In December of nineteen seventy, he starred in one of the strangest scenes in American history. He showed up at the White House, unannounced, and asked to speak with President Richard Nixon.

He wanted a badge as a federal narcotics agent.

Nixon received him. The photo of the two of them shaking hands is today one of the most requested images in the United States National Archives. And Elvis walked out of there with the badge.

The man whose body, years later, would be overrun with prescription pills wanted to help the government fight drugs.

Notice that contrast. Because the third most important character in this story hasn't even appeared yet. A doctor from Memphis who would enter Elvis's life with a pen and a prescription pad. And the Colonel, who controlled every contract, according to people who lived alongside both of them, did nothing to stop it.

And now you're finally going to understand what was behind all of this. Why the Colonel never went to Germany and turned down every offer from abroad. And why the biggest star on the planet never played a single concert in Europe.

The man the world knew as Colonel Tom Parker was not named Tom Parker.

And he was not born in the United States.

His real name was Andreas Cornelis van Kuijk. He was born on June twenty-sixth, nineteen oh nine, in Breda, a city in the south of the Netherlands.

In nineteen twenty-nine, at nineteen, he disappeared from the Netherlands without properly saying goodbye to anyone. He crossed the ocean and entered the United States illegally.

He adopted an American name. He invented an American past. He claimed to have been born in West Virginia.

And never, in his entire life, did he become a citizen of the United States.

Without citizenship, there was no way for him to get an American passport. And if he left the country, he ran the risk of not being able to get back in. Or of having his past investigated at the border.

This is where the second object in this story reveals itself completely. The passport the Colonel never had.

For the biographers who investigated the Colonel's life, this is the explanation for everything. That's why he never visited Elvis in Germany, and why no offer from abroad was ever accepted. And that's why Elvis Presley, the man millions of Europeans, Japanese, and South Americans dreamed of seeing, spent his whole life singing inside the borders of a single country.

The career of the biggest star on the planet was designed around an illegal immigrant's fear of being found out.

And there's a detail that's even more chilling. In nineteen sixty, Andreas's family in the Netherlands saw a photo of Elvis in a magazine. Standing next to the singer was his manager. And one of his sisters recognized the face instantly. It was the brother who had vanished thirty years earlier.

According to the biographers, one of his brothers even traveled to the United States to find him. And the Colonel made sure the secret stayed buried.

But that still isn't the Colonel's darkest secret. Because there is a question no biographer has been able to answer to this day. Why would a nineteen-year-old suddenly flee the Netherlands, without warning, in May of nineteen twenty-nine? And what happened in that same city, in that same month, may be the most disgusting answer in this entire story.

To understand what happened in Breda, you first need to know what Andreas did as soon as he arrived in the United States.

Because the first thing he did was hide inside a uniform.

In nineteen twenty-nine, freshly arrived, Andreas enlisted in the United States Army. And when it came time to give his name, he said it was Tom Parker.

According to records dug up later, the name was apparently borrowed from the very officer who interviewed him.

He served two years in an artillery unit in Hawaii. Then he enlisted again, in Florida.

There, something broke.

He walked away from his post without authorization. He was charged with desertion and thrown into solitary confinement. When he came out of isolation, he was so disturbed that he had to spend two months committed to a psychiatric hospital. Only then was he discharged.

Think about what that means. The man who, years later, would decide every step of Elvis Presley's life, who would sign multimillion-dollar contracts and even choose his wedding, had left the Army after a breakdown so severe that it ended in a psychiatric hospital. And as far as anyone knows, Elvis never knew any of this.

Over time, Tom Parker reinvented himself.

He worked in carnivals, learned to sell anything to anyone, and became a manager of singers. Until he found Elvis.

While he built an empire on the back of the kid from Memphis, his family in the Netherlands went on without any news. Andreas's mother died without ever knowing what had happened to her son.

She died in nineteen fifty-eight.

The same year Gladys Presley died.

The Colonel only learned of his own mother's death in April of nineteen sixty-one, when his brother Adam crossed the ocean to find him in Los Angeles.

Two men who lost their mothers in the same year. One wept beside the casket, in front of the entire country. The other only found out three years later, because he had erased his own family to hide who he was.

And now comes a figure that nobody outside the Colonel's circle knew. A Las Vegas number. A number that explains why the King sang twice a night, week after week, even when his body could no longer take it. You're going to hear that number in a moment.

The seventies arrived. And the world kept knocking on the door.

The Colonel found a way to give the world what it wanted without ever leaving the country.

On January fourteenth, nineteen seventy-three, Elvis performed a concert in Honolulu, Hawaii, broadcast by satellite. The special was called "Aloha from Hawaii."

The Colonel announced that a billion people would watch, and sold the show as the first entertainment special ever broadcast live to the whole world.

For the fans in Europe, Asia, and South America, it was their only chance to see the King sing. On television. And because of the time difference, many countries could only see the footage later, on tape.

Because live, right in front of them, Elvis was never going to go.

And notice the place they chose. Hawaii. The same place where, more than forty years earlier, a young Dutch immigrant had put on the uniform of the United States Army for the first time, using a name that wasn't his.

The invitations didn't stop.

In nineteen seventy-five, representatives from Saudi Arabia offered five million dollars for a series of performances. The Colonel refused. They doubled the offer. Ten million dollars.

Elvis wanted to go. The Colonel refused again.

Promoters from South America offered two and a half million dollars. The Colonel answered with a line that became famous: "Whenever I need two and a half million, I'll give you a call."

Ten million dollars turned down. And meanwhile, the man refusing fortunes abroad was sinking into debt inside the very hotel where Elvis was singing. It seems to make no sense. But it does. And when you understand it, you're going to feel sick.

While the Colonel was turning the world away, Elvis's personal life was falling apart.

In nineteen seventy-two, Priscilla left Graceland, taking Lisa Marie, who was four. The following year, the divorce was signed.

Elvis was left alone in a mansion full of people.

His friends from Memphis were still around him, day and night. But they were employees and bodyguards who depended on the salary he paid them. Few had the courage to contradict the boss.

And the boss was getting sicker and sicker.

He gained weight, lost weight, gained it again. He had intestinal problems and blood pressure problems. On stage, he sometimes forgot the lyrics and could barely stay on his feet.

He kept going on stage in Las Vegas. Twice a night.

But there was one night when Elvis tried to break free. And what happened in that hotel suite shows, better than any document, who really ran the King's life.

September third, nineteen seventy-three. The last show of a four-week engagement at the Las Vegas Hilton.

Days earlier, Elvis had learned that the hotel had fired an employee. A man he liked, whose wife was fighting cancer. According to the accounts, the employee was let go for having had contact with the singer, something the hotel's rules forbade.

Elvis was furious.

In the middle of the show, in front of the audience, he criticized the Hilton's management out loud, with profanity.

The Colonel was beside himself. The Hilton was where he did business and where he gambled. And his artist had just attacked the hotel in public.

In the early hours of that morning, in the suite on the thirtieth floor, the two of them had the biggest fight of all those years.

Elvis did what nobody expected.

He fired the Colonel.

For the first time in eighteen years, the King told the man who controlled his life that it was all over.

The Colonel shot back right away that he wanted out too. And he went straight to his office to prepare a bill.

A bill for everything that, according to him, Elvis still owed him.

The amount, according to the accounts, was somewhere between two and ten million dollars.

Elvis looked at that number and realized he had no way to pay it.

Within a few days, the two were working together again, as if nothing had happened.

Picture the scene. The most beloved man in the world tries to get rid of the person exploiting him, and discovers he can't even afford his own freedom. And there is a very concrete reason Elvis didn't have that money in that September. A reason that had been signed six months earlier.

The third object in this story is a stack of papers signed in March of nineteen seventy-three.

That year, the Colonel closed a deal with the RCA label that looked generous. RCA would pay five million, four hundred thousand dollars in one lump sum.

In exchange, it would keep the rights to every recording Elvis had made up to that point. All of them. "Heartbreak Hotel," "Hound Dog," "Love Me Tender," "Suspicious Minds." Everything he had recorded in almost twenty years of his career.

From then on, Elvis Presley would never again receive a single cent in royalties from those songs. Not him, and not his family, after his death.

And of the five million, four hundred thousand dollars, half went to the Colonel.

Half.

The man who had changed his name, who had fled his own country, kept half the money from the sale of everything Elvis Presley had ever sung. And Elvis, who needed money to keep up Graceland and pay for the divorce, signed.

And the second number is the one that explains why he needed that money so badly.

The Colonel was a gambling addict.

According to the Colonel's biographers, he was losing around a million dollars a year at the casino tables. And by the time Elvis died, his gambling debts to the Las Vegas Hilton were over thirty million dollars, according to those same accounts.

The same Hilton where Elvis sang twice a night.

Now put the pieces together. The Colonel turned down ten million dollars abroad because he couldn't leave the country. He needed money because he was losing fortunes at the roulette table. And the place where he lost those fortunes was exactly the hotel where he kept Elvis singing nonstop, show after show, even when the King was already sick.

While the King sang upstairs, his money went downstairs, onto the roulette table.

But the darkest piece is still missing. Because during those same years, a doctor from Memphis was handing Elvis quantities of pills that seem impossible today. And while that was happening, back in the Netherlands, the question from May of nineteen twenty-nine was still unanswered. And it will be answered before the end of this story.

His name was George Nichopoulos. In Memphis, everyone called him Dr. Nick.

He started treating Elvis in nineteen sixty-seven. By nineteen seventy, looking after the King had become practically his full-time job.

Dr. Nick traveled on the tours. He was on call at Graceland. And he always had on hand whatever Elvis asked for.

Pills to sleep after the show, and pills to wake up before the next one.

Years later, when the Tennessee medical authorities investigated the prescriptions, they found a number that seems impossible.

In the first eight months of nineteen seventy-seven, Dr. Nick prescribed Elvis Presley more than ten thousand doses of medication.

Ten thousand doses. In eight months.

Stimulants and sedatives, among many others. All of it by prescription, all of it with a doctor's stamp.

When he was questioned, Dr. Nick said he was trying to control Elvis's addiction. He even said he had a thousand fake pills made, with no effect at all, to fool his patient. And before the medical board, he summed it all up in one sentence: "I cared too much."

Remember that number. Ten thousand doses in eight months. And now think about the man who tracked every cent Elvis made, every show, every contract. The Colonel saw up close what state the King was in when he walked on stage. And the tours kept getting booked.

In July of nineteen seventy-six, Vernon Presley fired three of his son's longest-serving bodyguards. Red West, Sonny West, and Dave Hebler. Men who had been at Elvis's side for years.

Hurt and out of work, the three of them did something nobody in Elvis's circle had ever done.

They told everything in a book.

The book was called "Elvis: What Happened?" It described the pills, the breakdowns, the loneliness inside Graceland.

It was released on August first, nineteen seventy-seven.

Two weeks before Elvis died.

People close to him said later that Elvis was devastated by the betrayal. The men he considered brothers had exposed his life to the entire world.

And there is a date in Elvis's final months that seems written on purpose. A date that ties the last show of his life to the man who controlled him. And two years later, that same date would come back again.

June twenty-sixth, nineteen seventy-seven.

Indianapolis, Indiana.

Elvis walked onto the stage of a packed arena for what nobody knew would be the last show of his life.

He was bloated and exhausted. But he sang.

And here is the detail almost nobody connected. That very same day, June twenty-sixth, the Colonel was turning sixty-eight.

Andreas Cornelis van Kuijk had been born on a June twenty-sixth, in Breda. And the man he controlled played the last show of his life on his birthday.

Days earlier, the CBS television network had filmed two shows from that final tour for a special. The footage shows an Elvis unrecognizable to anyone who remembered the kid from nineteen fifty-six. Face swollen, sweat pouring down, the voice still powerful inside a body that seemed to be giving up.

The special wouldn't air until October. By the time the public finally saw those images, Elvis was already dead.

Elvis went back to Graceland. The next tour was scheduled to begin in August.

August sixteenth, nineteen seventy-seven.

Elvis stayed up all night, as always. He went to the dentist late in the evening and, before dawn, played racquetball with friends and played the piano.

In the morning, he told his fiancée, Ginger Alden, a twenty-year-old he had gotten engaged to that same year, that he was going to the bathroom to read for a while.

Early in the afternoon, Ginger woke up and realized Elvis still hadn't come back.

She knocked on the bathroom door. Nobody answered.

She opened the door.

Elvis was lying on the floor.

Ginger screamed. The staff came running up the stairs. An ambulance took Elvis to Baptist Memorial Hospital in Memphis, while the paramedics tried to revive him.

At three thirty in the afternoon, the doctors pronounced Elvis Aaron Presley dead.

He was forty-two years old.

Lisa Marie, nine years old, was at Graceland that day, spending some time with her father.

There was one more date hidden in that afternoon. Two days earlier, on August fourteenth, it had been nineteen years since Gladys died. The son who never got over losing his mother died almost on the same day she did.

The official cause released by the Memphis medical examiner was cardiac arrhythmia. But the toxicology tests done afterward, according to reports published in the years that followed, found more than ten different substances in his system, including tranquilizers, painkillers, and sedatives, some at levels far above normal. And the question about the role of those ten thousand doses never left the story again.

Imagine for a moment that Elvis was someone in your family. A son, a brother, a forty-two-year-old father, found alone on the bathroom floor. On the day Elvis died, the Colonel was in Portland, Maine, setting up the tour that was due to start the next day.

When he got the news, the accounts of his reaction vary. Some people remember an exclamation of shock. Others say he acted as if nothing had happened.

But there is one line recorded by the biographers that sums up the man.

When someone asked the Colonel what he would do now that Elvis was dead, he answered:

"Why, I'll just go right on managing him."

That is exactly what he did.

Before going to Graceland, the Colonel stopped in New York to meet with RCA executives and with the partners who sold merchandise with Elvis's image. With the King dead, demand for records, photos, and souvenirs was about to explode. And he wanted to be in control of all of it.

On the seventeenth, the casket was left open at Graceland so the fans could say goodbye. The line seemed to have no end.

Not even Elvis's death was safe from people who wanted to profit from him.

August eighteenth, nineteen seventy-seven. The funeral of Elvis Presley. A procession of white Cadillacs carried the King's body through the streets of Memphis to the cemetery, with fans weeping on the sidewalks the whole way.

The Colonel showed up at the funeral wearing a Hawaiian shirt.

According to people who were there, he stayed off in a corner, keeping his distance. And he used those days to convince Vernon, shattered by his son's death, to leave Elvis's business in his hands.

Vernon signed.

Mother and son, together again, in the house Elvis had bought for her. Later, his father, Vernon, and his grandmother, Minnie Mae, would also be buried there, in the same garden.

The Colonel's birthday. Again.

Elvis's last show and his father's death, on the same day that the man who had controlled the entire family's life was blowing out his candles.

And now it's time to answer the question that has been open since the beginning of the second half of this story. Why did Andreas van Kuijk flee the Netherlands in May of nineteen twenty-nine? Breda, the Netherlands. May seventeenth, nineteen twenty-nine.

In the back of a greengrocer's shop in the city, a twenty-three-year-old woman named Anna van den Enden was found dead. She had married the shop's owner only a short time before.

She had been beaten to death in the kitchen, behind the counter.

The killer left behind a detail that shocked the police. He scattered a thin layer of pepper around the body before fleeing. The suspicion is that he wanted to throw off the tracking dogs.

The crime was never solved.

It was precisely around that time that Andreas van Kuijk, then nineteen, disappeared.

According to what his own family told the American journalist Alanna Nash, who wrote the most complete biography of the Colonel, he left home suddenly, with no papers, no money, and no clothes.

He never went back to the Netherlands. Not to visit his mother's grave, and not after Elvis died, when there was no longer any career left to protect.

This is where honesty has to come before everything else.

There has never been any proof that Andreas had any connection to the death of Anna van den Enden. He was never investigated and never charged. Alanna Nash herself wrote that she could not claim he killed that woman, and that she was presenting the connection only as a possibility.

But the coincidence stayed forever. And when you look at it calmly, it's impossible not to feel a chill run down your spine.

A brutal crime in a small city. And, at the same time, a young man who vanishes without telling anyone, crosses the ocean, changes his name, hides inside a foreign uniform, and spends the rest of his life afraid to set foot outside the United States.

His family in the Netherlands, according to the same journalist, believed that if he did have some connection to that death, it would have been an accident. Maybe he was just a poor kid running from misery. Nobody can say for certain.

What is known is that his fear shaped the life of Elvis Presley.

And while that secret stayed buried, a twelve-year-old girl, Elvis's daughter, was about to discover, on paper, what the Colonel had done to her father. And that document is the final piece of this story.

Nineteen eighty.

Lisa Marie Presley was twelve. She was Elvis's only heir. And after Vernon's death, her father's fortune was being managed until she came of age.

But something caught the attention of the judge in charge of the case in Memphis, Joseph Evans. The Colonel was still collecting an enormous slice of everything Elvis's name brought in. And the heir was a child.

The judge appointed a lawyer to defend Lisa Marie's interests. His name was Blanchard Tual.

Tual's mission was simple to explain and hard to carry out. Examine everything the Colonel had done with Elvis's money.

This is where the fourth and final object in this story appears.

The Blanchard Tual report.

Tual dug into more than twenty years of contracts and agreements. From the first one, in nineteen fifty-five, to the fifty-percent deal and the papers from the nineteen seventy-three sale.

And he wrote down, in black and white, what many people had suspected for years.

The fifty-percent commission charged by the Colonel was described as excessive, far above what the industry charged, which ranged between fifteen and twenty percent.

And the sale of the song rights in nineteen seventy-three, the one that stripped Elvis and his family of every royalty from the biggest hits of his career, was called unethical and badly handled.

For the first time, an official document, prepared at the request of a court, put into words what the whole world had watched happen for twenty years.

Based on that report, the executors of Elvis's estate took the Colonel to court.

The case ended in a settlement in nineteen eighty-three. The Colonel received two million dollars and, in exchange, gave up the Elvis recordings and images he still controlled, and stayed out of any business tied to Elvis's name for five years.

Two million dollars. To walk away, the man who had kept half of everything got paid one more time.

Now look at the four pieces together.

The contract from nineteen fifty-five, signed by a young man who still needed his parents' signature.

The passport the Colonel never had, which locked the biggest star on the planet inside a single country.

The papers from nineteen seventy-three, which sold off Elvis's greatest hits in one stroke and put half the money in the manager's pocket.

And the report from nineteen eighty, which finally called all of it by its name.

That same year, he was criminally charged over excessive prescriptions for Elvis and other patients. The jury acquitted him. Only in nineteen ninety-three, after new complaints involving other patients, did he lose his medical license for good. He died in two thousand sixteen, at eighty-eight.

Four documents, and a single man on the other side of every signature.

The Colonel lived another twenty years after Elvis died.

He died in Las Vegas, on January twenty-first, nineteen ninety-seven, at eighty-seven, after a stroke.

Over his lifetime, he had earned more than a hundred million dollars. He left behind a little over one million. The rest fell away along the road, a good part of it on the gaming tables of Las Vegas.

In his final years, according to the biographers, he worked as a consultant for the same Hilton where he had lost so much money. It was a way to stay close to the tables.

Elvis's family, meanwhile, had to fight to keep from losing what was left.

After years of taxes, expenses, and bad contracts, the estate was far smaller than the world imagined. Keeping Graceland running cost a fortune.

It was Priscilla who found the way out. In June of nineteen eighty-two, Graceland opened its doors to the public as a museum. The fans came from every corner of the world, paying to see the house where the King had lived and died.

Graceland saved Elvis's estate. And the mansion he bought for his mother became one of the most visited homes in the United States, with hundreds of thousands of visitors a year.

Lisa Marie received her father's inheritance in nineteen ninety-three, when she turned twenty-five. She died in January of two thousand twenty-three, at fifty-four, and was buried at Graceland, next to her son, Benjamin, who had died three years earlier.

The Presley family is still tied to that house. And the name of the man who tried to control everything stayed outside the gates.

On Colonel Tom Parker's death certificate, the name written down was Andreas Cornelis van Kuijk. And the place of birth, the Netherlands.

After almost seventy years of hiding who he was, the secret that locked Elvis Presley inside an entire country only appeared in writing on an official document once the Colonel himself could no longer run from it.

Elvis never read that piece of paper. He died twenty years earlier and, as far as anyone knows, without ever learning who the man he had trusted with his own life really was.

In two thousand twenty-two, the movies told this story again for a new generation. Elvis grew up in a house that was missing a strong man, and he found a man who seemed strong enough to take care of everything. He lost his mother at twenty-three, and he stayed with whoever was closest. He handed his money and his freedom to someone he barely knew.
