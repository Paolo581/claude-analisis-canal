## INTRO

February twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-five. A maintenance worker looked through a window of a house in the hills outside Santa Fe, New Mexico, and called nine one one. The people inside the house were Gene Hackman, two-time Oscar winner, ninety-five years old, and his wife, Betsy Arakawa. Nobody had seen either of them in two weeks.

What the deputies found when they walked in has never been shown to the public. A judge sealed the photographs, and the family fought to keep them sealed.

But the written reports came out. And they describe a pacemaker that kept a record, a date on that record, and six days in which the most private movie star of his generation was alone in that house with his wife's body. He almost certainly never understood she was gone.

Stay with me until the end, because there is a clause in Betsy's will, written years earlier, that decided where everything went because of the order they died in. He missed it by eighty-four days.

To understand the man on the floor of that mudroom, you have to go back to a street in a small town in Illinois. To a boy watching a car drive away.

Danville, Illinois, nineteen forty-three. Eugene Allen Hackman was thirteen years old. His father, Eugene, ran the presses at the local newspaper. His mother, Lyda, had been a waitress. The family had moved around, lived in his grandmother's house, and never had much money.

One day, the boy was out playing in the street when his father drove past in the family car. His father didn't stop. He lifted one hand off the wheel and gave a small wave. And the boy understood, from that one gesture, that his father was leaving. He didn't come back.

Hackman told that story for sixty years. He said it taught him that one small movement could carry a whole lifetime of meaning, and that it was the moment that made him an actor. He never forgot the wave.

He said later that he'd joined to get away from home. He didn't say what he was getting away from. But he carried the Marines with him the rest of his life. In the way he stood. In the way he talked to directors. And in the temper that everyone who worked with him learned to respect.

Discharged in nineteen fifty-one, he bounced around. He studied journalism for a while on the G.I. Bill, took jobs in New York, worked as a doorman, a truck driver, a furniture mover, a soda jerk. Nothing stuck.

Years later, he told a story about the doorman job. He was standing outside a hotel in his uniform when a Marine sergeant he had served under walked past. The sergeant looked him up and down and said, Hackman, you're a sorry son of a gun. He never forgot that either.

Then, in nineteen fifty-six, at twenty-six, he did the thing everybody told him was crazy. He went to acting school, at the Pasadena Playhouse in California.

He was older than the other students, and he didn't look like a leading man. He had a thick neck, a receding hairline, and a face that looked like it had been in a few fights. The one student he got along with was a short, odd kid named Dustin Hoffman. At the end of the year, the two of them were voted the students least likely to succeed.

They moved to New York together, and for a while they shared an apartment with a third struggling actor named Robert Duvall. Three men who would win Oscars, sleeping on the floor and splitting the rent. None of them had a job.

In nineteen sixty-two, something happened back in Illinois that he almost never talked about. His mother died in a house fire. According to the accounts, it started with a cigarette she had been smoking. Hackman was thirty-two, broke, and trying to make it in New York.

He had married a bank secretary named Faye Maltese in nineteen fifty-six. They had three children, Christopher, Elizabeth and Leslie. And for years, he supported them with odd jobs while he took whatever parts he could get. Off-Broadway. Television bit parts. A summer-stock theater in upstate New York. He was in his thirties and still nobody.

The break came in nineteen sixty-seven. Warren Beatty had seen him in a small part and remembered him. He cast him as Buck Barrow, Clyde's older brother, in Bonnie and Clyde. He was thirty-seven. The part got him his first Oscar nomination.

Three years later, he got another one, for I Never Sang for My Father, playing a grown son who cannot make peace with a cold, demanding old man. People who knew his story noticed what he'd chosen to play.

Then came the role that made him a star. A rough, obsessive New York narcotics cop named Popeye Doyle, in The French Connection. The director, William Friedkin, didn't want him at first. He thought Hackman was too soft. Hackman spent weeks riding along with the real detective the character was based on, and came back meaner than Friedkin had asked for.

The car chase under the elevated train in Brooklyn was shot in real traffic, without permits, with Hackman doing some of the driving himself. It became one of the most famous scenes ever filmed. And in April of nineteen seventy-two, Gene Hackman, the kid voted least likely to succeed, walked up and took the Oscar for Best Actor.

He was forty-two. And from that moment on, he barely stopped working.

Hold on to the man in that chase scene, driving through red lights with both hands on the wheel. Fifty years later, the sheriff's detectives would go frame by frame through a gate camera looking for any car at all, and find nothing.

## PART 2

The nineteen seventies and eighties were Gene Hackman's decades. The Poseidon Adventure, where he was the priest who gives his life for strangers. Scarecrow, with Al Pacino. The Conversation, where he played a surveillance expert eaten alive by his own guilt. Night Moves. Superman, where he turned Lex Luthor into a comedian. Hoosiers, the small-town basketball coach half the country still quotes. Mississippi Burning, which got him another nomination.

He worked constantly. Some years he made three or four films. He said yes to good scripts and bad ones. He later admitted he took a lot of the bad ones for the money, because he'd grown up without any and never trusted that it would stay.

Directors loved him because he could do anything. Audiences trusted him because he looked like a real man. He wasn't handsome the way movie stars were handsome. He looked like your uncle, your boss, the cop at the end of the bar. And then he'd open his mouth and you couldn't look away.

But he was not easy. He had a temper, and he knew it. He fought with directors. He walked off sets. He once said that he'd been angry most of his life and that acting was the only place he could put it. On a set, the anger made him better. At home, it made him somebody his children tiptoed around.

But the work had a cost, and he paid it at home.

Hackman said himself, years later, that he had not been a good father. He was gone for months at a time. When he was home, he was tense and withdrawn. His son Christopher said in an interview that his father was hard to be around when he came back from a shoot. He'd bring the character home with him, and it would take days to wear off.

The boy who had watched his own father drive away had become a father who was never there. He saw it. He said so. He just couldn't seem to stop.

The marriage to Faye ended in nineteen eighty-six, after thirty years. The children were grown. And Hackman, by then in his mid-fifties, had already met the woman who would spend the rest of his life with him.

Her name was Betsy Arakawa. She was born in Hawaii, and she was a classical pianist. As a girl, she had been good enough to play with the Honolulu Symphony when she was eleven years old. She was about thirty years younger than he was. The story most often told is that they met in the early nineteen eighties at a fitness center in California. She was working part-time behind the desk to make ends meet.

Betsy was quiet. She did not want to be famous, and she never became famous. In forty years at the side of one of the most recognizable actors in the world, she almost never gave an interview. In Santa Fe, she co-owned a small home furnishings store downtown. The people who shopped there knew her as Betsy, not as Mrs. Hackman.

In nineteen ninety-one, the two of them got married. And around that time, Hackman made a decision that surprised Hollywood. He left it.

He bought land in the hills outside Santa Fe, New Mexico, and built a house there. Adobe walls, big windows, views of the mountains, at the end of a road inside a gated community where nobody bothered anybody. Far from Los Angeles, far from the cameras. He kept working, but he flew in, did the job, and flew home.

Keep that road in mind. The gate at the bottom of it, and the camera on that gate. They're the reason this story has dates on it at all.

The nineties were a second peak. The Firm. Crimson Tide, facing off against Denzel Washington in a submarine. Get Shorty. Enemy of the State, where he played, once again, a paranoid surveillance man hiding from the world. He was in his sixties and still the best thing in almost every movie he was in.

The next year, in nineteen ninety-two, Clint Eastwood cast him as Little Bill Daggett, the sheriff in Unforgiven. It won him his second Oscar. He was sixty-two.

And then came the role people under forty remember him for. Royal Tenenbaum, in two thousand one. A terrible father trying, too late, to win back his children. Hackman later said he hadn't enjoyed making it. The critics said it was one of the best things he ever did.

In two thousand four, he made a small comedy called Welcome to Mooseport. Almost nobody saw it. And it turned out to be the last film Gene Hackman ever made.

Twenty-one years later, the deputies who walked into his house had grown up watching him.

## PART 3

He didn't announce his retirement. He just stopped.

In two thousand eight, he finally explained why. He said he'd had a stress test, and the doctors told him his heart wasn't in the shape it needed to be for the strain of a film set. He said he'd rather walk away than die on a soundstage.

He was seventy-eight. And he had just started a second life.

Hackman wrote novels. Historical adventures, mostly, the kind of books he liked to read. He wrote three with a friend who was an underwater archaeologist, and then two on his own. He said writing was lonely, and that he liked it that way. He said nobody yelled cut.

In two thousand eleven, he gave one of his last interviews, to a magazine, and they asked him how he wanted to be remembered. He said, as a decent actor. As someone who tried to portray what was given to him in an honest fashion. That was all.

He painted. He sculpted. He rode his bike around Santa Fe. For a while he was part owner of a restaurant in town. He and Betsy ate at the same local places, and the people who worked there say he was polite, a little gruff, and never wanted to talk about movies. If a tourist recognized him, he'd nod and go back to his lunch.

In two thousand twelve, at eighty-two, he was hit by a car while riding his bicycle in the Florida Keys. He was knocked to the ground, taken to the hospital, and released. He got back on the bike.

That's the version of Gene Hackman the public had for twenty years. A tough old man who had walked away from Hollywood on his own terms and was enjoying his life in the desert.

What the public didn't know was what was happening inside his head.

At some point in his final years, Gene Hackman developed Alzheimer's disease. The family never announced it. There was no interview, no statement. The medical investigator would only confirm it after he was dead, and would call it advanced.

The last photographs of him were taken in March of twenty twenty-four, by a photographer who spotted him and Betsy having lunch at a restaurant in Santa Fe. He was ninety-four. He was thin and moved slowly, and he held on to Betsy's arm as he walked to the car. The pictures ran under headlines about how frail he looked.

What those pictures actually showed was a woman of sixty-four who had become the full-time caretaker of a man who could no longer take care of himself.

Betsy did everything. The shopping, the medications, the doctors' appointments, the dogs. The house was on a hill at the end of a gated road, and almost nobody came up it. According to the people who knew them, that was how they wanted it.

So where were his children? Far away. According to what the family said afterward, they hadn't spoken to their father in months. Not because of a fight. Because that was how it had always been.

So the structure of Gene Hackman's life, in his last years, rested on one person. If something happened to her, there was nobody else in the house. And nobody outside it who would know.

It was also what made what happened next possible.

## PART 4

Tuesday, February eleventh, twenty twenty-five.

This is the last day investigators could reconstruct Betsy Arakawa's movements, and they did it from her phone, her car and her receipts.

In the morning, she sent emails to her massage therapist. She went to a Sprouts grocery store, then to a CVS pharmacy, then to a pet supply store. She was back at the gate of the community at about a quarter past five in the afternoon. The gate camera recorded her car coming in.

After that, nothing. No more emails. No more texts. No more trips out.

For weeks, that's where the timeline ended. Then investigators found one more thing. On the morning of Wednesday, February twelfth, Betsy called a medical clinic in Santa Fe. She wanted an appointment. The clinic called her back that day, twice, and tried to set one up. Nobody answered.

That phone call is the last sign of Betsy Arakawa alive.

What was killing her had been in the air of her own property for who knows how long.

Hantavirus. It's carried by deer mice, and people catch it by breathing in dust from the droppings, urine or nests of infected rodents. It's rare. New Mexico sees a handful of cases a year. It starts like the flu. Fever, aches, exhaustion, sometimes for a week or more. Then, often very suddenly, the lungs fill with fluid. In New Mexico, between a third and almost half of the people who get hantavirus pulmonary syndrome die from it. Most of them die within a day or two of the lungs failing.

That's what the phone call on the twelfth means. Betsy felt sick enough to want a doctor. Not sick enough to go to the emergency room. She called the clinic in the morning. By the time they called back, she couldn't pick up.

Betsy was sixty-five and healthy. And by the medical investigator's reconstruction, she died on or around February twelfth, in the master bathroom of the house.

She was found on the floor near a space heater. On the counter above her was a prescription bottle, open, with pills scattered. The pills were thyroid medication. They had nothing to do with her death.

And in a closet a few feet from where she fell, there was a dog crate. Inside it was Zinna, an Australian kelpie Betsy had rescued and adored. Zinna had just had surgery, and she had been put in the crate to recover.

Nobody let her out. Zinna died of dehydration and starvation, in the crate, a few feet from the woman who had been coming back for her. The two other dogs had the run of the house. Zinna had a latch between her and the water bowl.

Somewhere else in that house, a ninety-five-year-old man with Alzheimer's was still alive.

## PART 5

What happened in the house between February twelfth and February eighteenth is the part of this story that nobody can fully tell. There were no witnesses. The only witness who could talk couldn't remember. And the only record is a medical device in his chest.

Back at the start of this video, I set aside a pacemaker. This is it. Gene Hackman had a pacemaker, and a pacemaker keeps a log. When investigators pulled the data, it showed his heart in a normal rhythm on Monday, February seventeenth. On Tuesday, February eighteenth, it recorded an abnormal rhythm. After that, nothing.

Six days after his wife, the man who could no longer take care of himself died. The medical investigator said the cause was heart disease, severe, with the advanced Alzheimer's as a significant contributing factor. His kidneys were failing too.

And then she said the thing that turned a sad story into an unbearable one.

His stomach was empty. He had not eaten in a long time. He was not dehydrated, so he had found water. But he had gone days without food, in a house full of it. And in the medical investigator's judgment, given the state of his mind, it was quite possible he never understood that his wife was dead.

He was found in a mudroom off the kitchen, on the floor. His cane was next to him. So were his sunglasses. The investigators believe he fell there, maybe on his way to the door, maybe on his way back from it.

The deputies' reports describe him in gray sweatpants and slippers. The clothes of a man at home, on an ordinary day, who had gotten up and gotten dressed the way he always did.

And then, for eight more days, nobody came.

## PART 6

Wednesday, February twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-five. Early afternoon.

A maintenance worker who had done jobs on the property for years was worried. He'd been trying to reach Betsy for two weeks, and Betsy always answered. He drove up with a security guard from the gated community. The doors were locked. They walked around the house, and through a window, they saw a body on the floor.

The nine one one call was released later. The man on the phone is struggling. He tells the dispatcher there are two people inside who aren't moving. She asks if they're breathing. He says he has no idea. She asks for the address. He says he can't give her one, that he's at the Hackman place, and he starts to cry.

The deputies from the Santa Fe County Sheriff's Office went in with their body cameras on.

The front door was unlocked. They found Betsy in the bathroom, Zinna in the crate, and Gene in the mudroom. Both bodies showed signs of having been there a long time. The reports use a clinical word for it. Mummification. The dry air of the high desert had done to them what it does to everything. The two living dogs were running loose. There was no sign of a struggle, nothing taken, no forced entry.

The first thing the deputies did was call the gas company, because two people and a dog dead in a closed house in February looks, at first, like carbon monoxide. The gas company checked. The levels were normal. The heaters were fine.

So the sheriff, Adan Mendoza, said in the first press conference what everyone was thinking. The circumstances were suspicious enough to require a full investigation. Two people dead in the same house, days apart, with no obvious cause, and a famous name on the mailbox.

His detectives got a search warrant. They took the phones, the computers, the pill bottle, the pacemaker data. They pulled the gate camera footage and went through it frame by frame, looking for a car that came or went between the eleventh and the twenty-sixth. They talked to the maintenance workers, the neighbors, the clinic. They built the timeline backward from the two bodies, one day at a time.

For two weeks, the theories ran wild. Murder-suicide. A gas leak the company missed. Poisoning. The scattered pills. Online, people built elaborate plots out of the fact that a famous man had died and nobody could yet say why.

The sheriff asked for patience. He said the autopsies would take time, and the toxicology longer. He said that whatever had happened in that house, it had happened days or weeks before anyone knew, and that the timeline was the key to everything.

Then, on March seventh, the chief medical investigator for the State of New Mexico stood in front of the cameras and laid out what the bodies had told her.

Betsy, hantavirus pulmonary syndrome, around February twelfth. Gene, heart disease with advanced Alzheimer's, around February eighteenth. No carbon monoxide in either body. No trauma. The pills were hers, and they were not the cause. Natural causes, both of them.

A reporter asked the medical investigator the question everyone wanted answered. Did he know his wife was dead? She chose her words carefully. She said she could not speak to his state of mind. But she said that given the advanced stage of his Alzheimer's, it was quite possible he was not aware that she had died.

Another reporter asked why he hadn't eaten. She said there was no food in his stomach, but no signs of dehydration either. She could not say what he had or hadn't done in those days. Nobody could. That's the hole in the middle of this story, and it will never be filled.

The following month, the State Department of Health sent inspectors to walk the property. The house itself was clean. But in the detached garages, the sheds, the guest houses and three old vehicles parked on the land, they found what they were looking for. Rodent nests, droppings, live mice and dead ones. Eight outbuildings, and every one of them had signs of mice.

Betsy had almost certainly breathed in the virus somewhere on her own land, doing something ordinary. Opening a shed. Cleaning out a garage. Starting a car that had been sitting.

## PART 7

By the end of March, the case was closed. And the fight over what the public would be allowed to see had already started.

Under New Mexico law, the sheriff's reports, the body camera footage and the photographs from inside the house were public records. News organizations asked for them. And the Hackman estate went to court to stop it.

The lawyer for the estate argued that the family had a right to grieve in private. He said footage of two people who had died alone, days apart, in that condition, served no public purpose. He pointed out that the couple had spent thirty years avoiding the public eye on purpose.

The sheriff's office said it was prepared to follow the law and release everything, if the court said so. The news organizations argued that the public had a right to see how a case this big had been handled.

On March thirty-first, a judge in Santa Fe ruled. The written reports could be released. The body camera video could be released, with the bodies blurred or cut. But the photographs and video showing the bodies of Gene Hackman and Betsy Arakawa were sealed. They cannot be published. The judge said the family's privacy outweighed the public's interest in seeing them.

That is the order. That is why the headlines say the photos are locked away. Not because of what's in them that's mysterious. Because of what's in them that's unbearable, and because the people who loved them asked.

What was released was enough. The body camera video shows the deputies walking through the rooms, calling out, getting no answer. It shows a kitchen with dishes in it, a house that looked lived in. The investigators' notes describe the dog door the surviving dogs were using to get out to the yard and back. The reports describe the space heater, the pills, the crate, the cane.

And they describe the thing the deputies kept coming back to. Nothing in that house was wrong, except that the two people who lived in it were dead.

And the probate filings described something else.

Gene Hackman's estate was estimated at around eighty million dollars. According to the court filings reported at the time, his will left everything to Betsy. Betsy's will left everything to him.

But Betsy's will had a clause. If her husband did not survive her by ninety days, her estate would not go to him or to his family. It would go to charity.

He survived her by six.

So on paper, the order they died in, days apart, in the same house, decided where her money went. And it left a question for the lawyers about his three children. They were not named in the will that was made public, and what they would receive became the subject of weeks of reporting.

The children said very little. Their statement, when it came, was short. He was loved and admired by millions around the world for his brilliant acting career, they wrote. But to us, he was always just Dad and Grandpa. They said they were devastated, and they asked for privacy.

His daughter Elizabeth told a reporter, in those first days, that she had not spoken to her father in a couple of months. She said that was normal. She said he was happy, and that the two of them, her father and Betsy, had a good life out there in the desert, away from everything.

The two dogs who survived, Bear and Nikita, were taken in by a friend of the couple who runs a dog rescue in Santa Fe. He said Bear had been found lying near Betsy, and would not leave her. Nikita had been found outside.

## PART 8

When the news broke, the tributes came from the people who had worked with him. Clint Eastwood, who had directed him to his second Oscar and was ninety-four himself, said there was no finer actor than Gene. Intense and instinctive, he said. Never a false note. Francis Ford Coppola, who had directed him in The Conversation, called him a great artist, inspiring and magnificent in his work and complexity.

And then the world did what it always does. It moved on. And in November of twenty twenty-five, the things Gene Hackman had surrounded himself with for thirty years went up for auction in New York. Furniture, art he'd collected, scripts, his own paintings. Landscapes, mostly. Desert, light, the hills around the house.

People lined up to see them. They wanted to own something that had been in that house. A chair he'd sat in. A painting he'd made with his own hands in the years when the world had stopped seeing him.

The Oscars were not for sale. Those stay with the family. What sold was the ordinary stuff of a long life. The things a man and a woman accumulate when they stay in one house for thirty years and don't expect anyone to come look.

And when you put it all together, the arc of his life, the ending starts to look less like a mystery and more like a shape.

A boy in Danville watched his father's hand go up in a car window and drive away. He spent the rest of his life playing men who were left, men who left, men who couldn't say what they felt. He told interviewers the wave was the moment that made him an actor, because it taught him how much a small gesture could hold.

And then he grew old and lost his memory. And the one person who had stayed, who had not driven away, died on the floor of a bathroom twenty feet from him. And he didn't know. He walked around that house for six days, waiting for someone who was never coming back, and he couldn't have told you why.

The boy who learned everything about loss from a hand waving goodbye became the old man who lost the only person who never left, and never knew she was gone.

That is the disturbing thing about Gene Hackman's house. Not what anybody did. Nobody did anything. There was no killer and no secret room and no plot. There was a virus in a garage. A heart that was already failing. A mind that had already gone. And a woman who had been holding all of it together by herself, at the end of a road nobody drove up.

The judge sealed the photographs. The family asked, and the court agreed, and they will stay sealed.

But the reports are public. And they tell you everything you need to know. Two people who spent thirty years keeping the world out succeeded so well that when they needed the world, it took sixteen days to arrive. Up that road, past that gate, past the camera that recorded her car coming home on the eleventh and nothing else, ever again.
