# Lawrence Welk

*Cold open (archive clip):* "PBS now returns to another mildly enjoyable moment from the Lawrence Welk Show. For anyone who grew up in the 50s or 60s, his show was a staple, usually watched by mom or dad, or requested maybe by grandma when she came to visit. The Lawrence Welk Show and the music played by the band were classic American songs."

## 1. Introduction

The 11th of March, 1924. A farm outside Strasburg, North Dakota, about 60 miles south of Bismarck, in country that had been broken out of open prairie inside living memory. A young man turns 21 years old that morning, and he leaves.

He is carrying an accordion that he has spent the last four years paying for, not in money, but in labor, under the terms of a contract he offered his own father when he was 17. He has $3 pinned to the inside of his jacket. He has a fourth-grade education, and he has an extremely limited grasp of the English language, because until that year of his life, he had barely spoken a word of it.

Here is what that young man was walking toward. Thirty-one years on American television, from 1951 to 1982. Sixteen of them on a national network in prime time. Eleven more after that on a distribution system he built himself, reaching a reported audience of as many as 30 million people a week. The longest-running musical variety program in the history of the medium, still being broadcast on public television stations more than a hundred years after he was born.

His name was Lawrence Welk. And for three decades, tens of millions of Americans watched him every week and understood almost nothing about who they were actually looking at.

You know the picture: the bubble machine, the matching outfits, the champagne music, a courtly, beaming man in a dinner jacket counting a band in with an accent that half the country could imitate and the other half found faintly ridiculous. He was, for the whole of the 1960s and 1970s, the single easiest target on television. Critics used him as shorthand for everything unhip in America. Comedians did him. Younger audiences groaned when their parents put him on. And essentially none of them understood what they were looking at.

The accent everyone imitated was not a performance, and the reason for it is far stranger than anybody assumed. The homespun farm boy signed a four-year contract with his own father at 17 and honored every day of it. The famous "musical family" was not a slogan, but an actual financial structure, and it is why people stayed with him for 30 years. And the man the industry treated as a harmless relic did something in 1971 that no network executive saw coming, and very few entertainers in history have managed at all.

If Saturday night in your house ever meant that bubble machine, a tap on the like button puts him in front of somebody else who remembers it.

It begins in a house made of sod.

## 2. Early life in North Dakota

Lawrence Welk was born on the 11th of March, 1903, in a farmhouse near Strasburg, in Emmons County, North Dakota. The walls were wood-sided and packed with sod, which is what people built with on that prairie when there was no timber and no money.

His parents were Ludwig and Christina Welk. They were Roman Catholics, and they were ethnic Germans, and they had come to America in the early 1890s from the Kutschurgan district near Odessa, in what was then the Russian Empire and is now Ukraine. Their own families had gone east into Russia from Alsace generations earlier, which is a migration most Americans have never heard of, and which explains a great deal about this story.

They arrived with nothing, and they homesteaded. The family story, which has been told many times since, is that their first winter in America was spent underneath an overturned wagon covered over with sod.

Understand what that sentence actually describes. A family crossed an ocean and then most of a continent, arrived on open prairie in the early 1890s with no house and no timber to build one, turned their wagon upside down, packed earth over it, and lived in it until spring.

Lawrence was the sixth of eight children born into that. Emmons County in 1903 was not the frontier of a western film, and it was not comfortable either. It was hard agricultural country with brutal winters, worked by immigrant families who had been given land on the condition that they survive on it. The Welks farmed, and Ludwig also worked as a blacksmith. And every child in that house worked as soon as the child could be useful.

And here is the first thing that almost nobody watching him in 1965 had any idea about. That household did not speak English. It spoke German. Not as a second language at the dinner table, but as the entire operating language of the family, the farm, and the community around it. Strasburg was a German-speaking settlement. The church was German. The school Lawrence attended was a parochial school staffed by German-speaking nuns.

He left that school after the fourth grade. Fourth grade. Think about the arithmetic of that against what he became. The man who would eventually run a national television production, negotiate with a network, manage a payroll of dozens of performers, and design a profit-sharing scheme stopped his formal education at about nine years old.

Part of that was the farm, because a family of eight children on a homestead needs hands more than it needs graduates. And part of it was that at about ten years old, Lawrence Welk nearly died. His appendix ruptured. In Emmons County in roughly 1913, that was very close to a death sentence, and he had to be got to Bismarck, sixty-odd miles away, for treatment. He survived it, and the recovery was long, and he spent a great deal of that convalescence at home with an instrument in his hands.

So the two facts that shaped his entire life happened in the same stretch of his childhood, and they happened together. He lost his formal education, and in the long, empty months of getting well, he found the instrument. There is a version of that boy who recovers, goes back to school, and becomes a farmer in Emmons County like his brothers. That version almost certainly existed until his appendix ruptured.

And the language stayed German. He would tell interviewers for the rest of his career that he did not learn English until he was about 21 years old.

Sit with that against everything you remember about him. For 31 years on American television, audiences heard that accent and assumed it was a bit, a folksy affectation, a man from the sticks laying it on for the cameras the way a country comedian does. Critics wrote about the malapropisms and the mangled introductions as though they were part of an act. They were not an act. They were a man performing live in front of tens of millions of people in his second language, having started to learn it as an adult with a fourth-grade education behind him.

Try the experiment in reverse. Take any performer you can think of, put them in front of 30 million people every week, and require them to host live: introduce guests by name, ad-lib with a studio audience, and read cue cards in a language they began learning at 21, with a fourth-grade education behind them. Almost nobody could do it. He did it for 31 years.

The single most mocked thing about Lawrence Welk was the one thing about him that nobody doing the mocking had earned the right to laugh at.

## 3. The $400 contract

The music came from his father. Ludwig Welk had carried an accordion with him from the old country, which tells you something about what a family chooses when it can take almost nothing. It came across an ocean and then across a continent by ox cart to a sod house in North Dakota. And in the evenings, the father played it.

The son was transfixed. Lawrence saved what pennies he could get hold of and bought himself a cheap mail-order accordion, and he played it until it fell apart. By his teens, he was earning money at barn dances, weddings, and whatever else the county offered, from about the age of 13.

But a mail-order instrument will only take a serious player so far. And by 17, he wanted a professional one, and a professional one cost $400. Four hundred dollars on a Dakota homestead in 1920 was an enormous sum. It was not money the Welks had spare, and it was not money they were going to hand a teenager for something as frivolous as a career in music, which, as far as Ludwig and Christina were concerned, was not a career at all.

So Lawrence made them an offer. He proposed that his father put up the $400, and in exchange he would stay and work the family farm until his 21st birthday. Four years. And on top of that, every dollar he earned playing dances during those four years would go into the family pot rather than his own pocket.

They accepted.

It is worth pausing on what each side was actually agreeing to. Ludwig and Christina were putting up a sum of money they could ill afford on an instrument for a purpose they did not believe in, and getting back four years of guaranteed labor from their strongest teenage son, plus whatever he could earn on the side. From a farming point of view, it was not a bad trade at all. And Lawrence was agreeing to hand over his late adolescence in exchange for one object and one shot.

Now, an enormous number of young people have made a bargain like that with a parent. And most of those bargains fall apart somewhere in the second year, because 17-year-olds who want to be musicians are not famous for their patience.

Lawrence Welk kept every word of it. He worked that farm for four years. He played the dances. He handed over the money. He did not renegotiate it. He did not leave early. And he did not, by any account that survives, complain about it.

And then, on the 11th of March, 1924, the day he turned 21 and the contract expired, he walked off the property. He took the accordion he had paid for. He had $3 pinned inside a new jacket. He had a fourth-grade education, no English worth speaking of, no contacts, no plan beyond the general direction of anywhere else. And by every account, his family thought he was making a serious mistake.

That is the second thing the audience never knew, and it reframes everything about him. The man America decided was a genial simpleton had, at 17, identified exactly what he wanted, worked out precisely what it would cost, negotiated the terms himself, and then delivered four years of hard labor to the day in order to own one object outright. There is not a great deal in the whole record of American show business that is more disciplined than that.

And notice what it says about how he thought. He did not run away, which is what the story usually is. He did not borrow the money from somebody else, or beg, or sulk. He looked at a thing he wanted, worked out what the person holding it would consider a fair exchange, offered terms generous enough that refusing would have been unreasonable, and then delivered on them completely, so that nobody could ever say he had not.

That is not a farm boy's instinct. That is a negotiator's instinct. And he had it at 17, in his second language, with four years of schooling.

## 4. Apprenticeship and big bands

What followed was 13 years of the hardest apprenticeship the entertainment business had to offer. And he did all of it before most people had heard his name.

He played wherever there was work, and in the beginning, the work was barn dances and weddings and small-town halls, which is to say he was a laborer with an accordion rather than an artist with a career.

In 1926, he caught on with a small traveling outfit called the Peerless Entertainers, run by a man named George T. Kelly. And Welk spent the rest of his life crediting Kelly as the person who actually taught him the trade. Not the music. The business: booking, advertising, showmanship, how to read a room, and how to get along with every kind of person a traveling show runs into, from theater managers to drunks.

For a young man with limited English, no social polish, and no formal education at all, that apprenticeship was worth more than any conservatory in America. And he knew it, and said so for 60 years.

Then he started forming his own groups, and the names they went through tell you exactly what the era was like: the Lawrence Welk Novelty Orchestra, the Biggest Little Band in America, the Hotsy Totsy Boys, and, after he landed a sponsorship, the Honolulu Fruit Gum Orchestra, which is a name no bandleader has ever been able to live down.

In 1927, the group set out for New Orleans to escape a North Dakota winter and never got further than Yankton, South Dakota. They auditioned at a local radio station called WNAX. The audition went out live, and it went well enough that WNAX gave them a regular program.

That radio slot ran into the 1930s, and it changed everything, because radio in the rural Midwest in that decade was not one medium among several. It was the medium. It was how a farm family in South Dakota found out what was happening anywhere. A band with a regular WNAX show could fill ballrooms across four states. And they did.

They bought a tour bus to carry the growing outfit. They worked the Tom Archer ballrooms across Iowa, Nebraska, South Dakota, and Missouri. The sponsors were what you would expect of that market, which is to say the band read commercials for hog tonic and agricultural supplies between numbers.

For the better part of a decade, through the worst years of the Depression, Lawrence Welk was what the business called a territory bandleader. That meant a working musician running a small traveling business, meeting a payroll, booking his own dates, and driving between towns that in the 1930s were emptying out. He was doing it through the Dust Bowl years, in the exact part of America the Dust Bowl hit, playing dance music to people who had very little left to spend on dancing.

And in Yankton, he met a nurse. Fern Renner was working at Sacred Heart Hospital there, and Lawrence courted her, and they were married in 1931. He was 28. That marriage lasted 61 years, until the night he died. There were three children: Shirley, born in 1932, Donna in 1937, and Lawrence Jr. in 1940.

Hold on to that, because when we get to the end of this video, it is the number that matters most. And because of what it meant practically: he was on the road almost continuously through the 1930s. He married a woman with a serious profession of her own, at a moment when a nurse's training was one of the few genuine careers available to a woman in South Dakota, and she took on the raising of three children largely around a husband who was working four states at a time.

By the late 1930s, he had moved his base of operations to Omaha, and the band had grown to ten pieces, and he was pushing east and up.

The name came in 1938, in Pittsburgh, and it came from the audience rather than from him. People began describing the sound of his band as being like sipping champagne: light, bubbly, effervescent. He took the compliment and he built an entire identity out of it. From then on, it was champagne music, and eventually there would be a machine on the stage producing actual bubbles.

That decision tells you as much about Welk as the $400 contract does. He was in the middle of the big band era, competing against orchestras led by some of the finest musicians in America. And he understood something most of them did not. He was never going to outplay Ellington or outswing Goodman. What he could do was be completely, unmistakably identifiable within four bars, be pleasant to spend an evening with, and never once make the audience feel stupid.

He got the Trianon and the Aragon ballrooms in Chicago, which in that era were among the most important rooms in the country and a long way from a hall in Yankton. He recorded, he toured, and by 1950 he had been a professional musician for 26 years, and he was, by any reasonable measure, a successful bandleader working in a form that was already dying underneath him.

That is the part worth sitting with. The big band era was finishing. The ballrooms were closing across the country. Television was arriving, and the orchestras that had defined American popular music for 20 years were breaking up, because there was no longer an economy to support 14 musicians on a bus. Most of Welk's contemporaries either scaled down, took house jobs, or simply stopped.

He was 47 years old, in a dying business, with a fourth-grade education. And then, in 1951, he moved to Los Angeles.

## 5. The Lawrence Welk Show

On the 2nd of May that year, his band appeared on KTLA, a local television station. He was 48 years old, and he had just found the thing he was actually built for.

The local show was a hit almost immediately, and it ran for four years. And in that time, Welk learned the new medium in a way that a great many far more celebrated performers of the period never bothered to. Television was small and intimate and relentless. It did not reward virtuosity the way a concert hall did. It rewarded being someone an audience was willing to have in the house every single week without getting tired of you, which is a completely different skill and a much rarer one.

He had it. Consistency, warmth, no edge whatsoever, and a face that looked genuinely pleased to see you. He also had 26 years of reading rooms behind him, courtesy of George T. Kelly, and a band he had trained to be identifiable inside four bars. Everything he had learned since 1924 turned out to be preparation for a medium that had not existed when he started.

On the 2nd of July, 1955, the American Broadcasting Company put the Lawrence Welk Show on the national network. What happened over the next 16 years is one of the most durable runs in the history of American broadcasting.

The show went out weekly into millions of homes, in a country with three networks and no way to watch anything later. It was aggressively wholesome, and it was that way on purpose. And by the middle 1960s, that positioning had made it something close to an institution among older and more conservative audiences who were finding the rest of the culture increasingly unrecognizable.

He built it around people rather than around material. And that decision is the single most underrated thing about the whole operation. Most variety programs of that era were built around a host and a rotating guest list. The audience tuned in for whoever was on that week. Welk did close to the opposite. He assembled a permanent company and let the audience watch it over years, which meant viewers were not following a show so much as following a group of people they came to feel they knew.

Myron Floren, the accordionist who became his second-in-command, had joined in 1950, before the network years. His personal manager, Sam Lutz, came in 1952. His musical director, George Cates, arrived in 1956. The Lennon Sisters, four girls from a Catholic family in Venice, California, came on in 1956 and became the first of a long line of young performers the audience watched grow up on that stage over the following 21 years.

He called them his musical family, and every critic in America treated that phrase as marketing. Here is what was actually underneath it, and it is the thing that says most about him.

Lawrence Welk paid union scale. That was his policy from the beginning, and he never changed it. And it meant that, on paper, a good musician could earn considerably more somewhere else. Plenty of leaders in that era paid over scale to hold on to talent. What Welk did instead, starting in 1956, was set up a profit-sharing arrangement, and he weighted it by how long a person had been with the organization.

Think about what that does. It converts a job into a stake. It rewards the thing that is hardest to buy, which is somebody staying. And it means the longer you are there, the more expensive it becomes to leave. Not because you are trapped, but because you own a piece of what you have helped build.

Now look at the result.

Myron Floren joined in 1950 and was still there in 1982. Sam Lutz joined in 1952 and was still there in 1982. George Cates joined in 1956 and was still there in 1982.

That is three men at the center of the operation giving 30 years, 26 years, and 26 years of their working lives to one employer, in an industry where nobody stays anywhere. The man with the fourth-grade education, whose entire public image rested on not being sophisticated, had designed a compensation structure that solved the single hardest problem in show business. And he did it in 1956, decades before that kind of thinking became standard anywhere.

And it produced the thing the audience actually responded to without ever being able to name it. When Welk called those people his musical family on air, it did not sound like a slogan, because it was not one. The viewers at home were watching a group of performers who had genuinely been together for 20 years and expected to be together for 20 more. And that comes through a camera whether or not anybody understands why.

The critics never wrote about that once. They were busy with the accent.

## 6. The Rural Purge and the comeback

And it is worth being honest about how bad the mockery got, because he absorbed it for the better part of 30 years. Through the 1960s in particular, as the culture went one direction at speed, Lawrence Welk became the standing joke for everything square, safe, and old in American life. He was parodied constantly. His name was a punchline that required no setup. Younger audiences used him as shorthand for whatever their grandparents liked. And critics who would never have written that way about a jazz musician wrote it about him weekly.

He knew all of it. He was not insulated from it, and he was not stupid about it. And he never changed one thing about the program in response. No attempt to get hip, no reinvention, no younger co-host, no rock number wedged in to prove he was keeping up. He had an audience. He knew exactly who they were, and he declined to be embarrassed about them. There is a considerable amount of quiet steel in that.

Then, in 1971, ABC cancelled him.

It was not really about him. What happened that year has a name in television history, and it is called the Rural Purge. The networks looked at their audience data, decided that older and more rural viewers were not the demographic advertisers were paying for, and cleared them out in a single sweep. The Beverly Hillbillies went. Green Acres went. Petticoat Junction went. And so did the Lawrence Welk Show, after 16 years on the network.

He was 68 years old. He had been a working musician since 1924, which is 47 years, and he had been on national television for 16. Every reasonable expectation was that this was the end. He would tour a little, take the honors, and retire gracefully somewhere warm, because a network cancellation in 1971 was about as final as a verdict got. There were three networks in America. If none of them wanted you, there was no other door to try.

So Lawrence Welk built one.

And the thing to understand about what he did next is that in 1971, it was not an obvious move that somebody had already proved. First-run syndication of an expensive weekly variety show, sold station by station across the country, was not how the business worked. The networks distributed programming. That was their entire function and their entire power.

Working with his producer, he took a proposition out to individual television stations across the country. He would supply the show. The stations could have it, and in exchange, he would keep a few minutes of national advertising inside it, which his own organization would sell.

In September 1971, months after ABC had dropped him, the Lawrence Welk Show returned in syndication on more than 200 stations.

That number needs stating twice, because it is the whole point. More than 200 stations, which was more than ABC itself had affiliates at the time. The network cancelled him for being obsolete, and within a matter of months, he was on more television stations than the network that cancelled him.

And he did not merely survive out there, which is what usually happens to a cancelled show that limps into syndication. Reports of the syndicated run put his audience as high as 30 million people a week, which was larger than what he had been drawing on the network that let him go. The advertisers ABC could not sell him to turned out to exist in very large numbers. They had simply been counted as the wrong sort of viewer.

He kept producing brand-new programs for another 11 years, until he stopped in 1982, at 79 years old.

Consider the shape of that. A national network told a 68-year-old man that his audience was worthless, and he spent the following decade proving it was 30 million people deep, using a distribution system he assembled himself out of individual television stations. By then, the Lawrence Welk Show was the longest-running musical variety program in the history of American television. And roughly a third of that run happened after a national network decided he was finished.

That is the fourth thing the audience never knew. The man who looked like the least commercially sophisticated person on television, the accordion player with the funny accent and the bubble machine, had responded to being cancelled by quietly building his own distribution network and beating the people who cancelled him with it. Nobody who watched that show on a Saturday night had the faintest idea they were watching the work of one of the shrewdest operators the medium ever produced.

He stopped making new programs in 1982, and the show did not stop. The reruns kept going, and in 1987, public television stations began broadcasting them to a whole new audience, and they have essentially never stopped since.

## 7. Legacy of a simple man

There are people who found Lawrence Welk in the 1990s, and in the 2010s, and this year, decades after the last program was recorded, sitting down to a bubble machine and a band in matching jackets on a Saturday evening. That is an extraordinarily strange afterlife for a television program, and it is worth naming why it happened.

Almost nothing made for television in the 1960s survives in continuous broadcast. Variety shows in particular date terribly, because they are built out of whoever was famous that month. Welk's did not date, for the same reason it was mocked at the time. It was never tied to a moment. There was nothing topical in it, nothing fashionable, no guest stars whose fame would expire. A program built deliberately out of nothing contemporary turned out to be the one thing from that decade that never became a period piece.

He had other interests in his last years. There was the resort community he established at Escondido, in Southern California, which became a place a good many of his viewers eventually went. There was a family organization running the various parts of what he had built, and his children, and eventually his grandchildren, came into it.

He also kept appearing. He introduced the rebroadcasts. He turned up at reunions of the cast. He went back to North Dakota, and the state has claimed him as its favorite son ever since. And the homestead outside Strasburg where the whole thing started is preserved now as a historic site. The sod-walled house is still standing on that prairie. You can go and look at the room.

But the thing that stayed constant was the same thing that had been constant since Yankton in 1931. Fern.

They had been married for 50 years by 1981, and neither of them appears to have found that remarkable. And there is no record of anything else. No separations, no public difficulty, nothing in 61 years that any columnist was ever able to turn into a story, which, for a man who was on national television every week from 1955 and surrounded by young performers for 27 years, in an industry that generates scandal as a byproduct, is close to remarkable on its own.

He had been suffering the effects of pneumonia, and he did not recover from it. Lawrence Welk died at about seven on the evening of Sunday, the 17th of May, 1992, at his home in Santa Monica, California. He was 89 years old. Fern was at his side. They had been married 61 years. Their son Larry was there, and their daughter Shirley.

He was buried at Holy Cross Cemetery in Culver City. The funeral was private, which is exactly the choice you would expect from him.

He had been performing professionally in one form or another from the barn dances of about 1916 to his retirement in 1982. That is roughly 66 years of continuous working life, undertaken by a man who left school at about nine years old, in a country whose language he did not speak until he was 21. He outlived the ballroom circuit, the big band era, the collapse of the variety format, and the network that cancelled him. The show was still on the air when he died, and it is still on the air now.

## 8. Conclusion

So here is the honest summary, and it is not what the title of this video suggests.

There were no scandals. Not one. No arrests, no disgrace, no drinking stories, no messy divorce, nothing in 66 working years and 31 years on national television that ever put Lawrence Welk in a newspaper for anything other than his work. He married one woman in 1931 and was still married to her the night he died in 1992. His musicians stayed 30 years. His producers stayed 30 years.

The double life was not a vice, and it was not a hidden identity. It was that America spent three decades watching a man it had decided was simple. And he was not simple.

He was a German-speaking farm child from a sod house who taught himself English as an adult, and then went on television in it, in front of the whole country, for 31 years, and let them laugh at how he said the words. He was a 17-year-old who wrote his own four-year contract and honored every day of it. He was an employer who worked out in 1956 how to make people want to stay, and then kept them for three decades in a business where nobody stays anywhere. He was a husband who married a nurse in Yankton, South Dakota, in 1931 and was still married to her 61 years later, on the last night of his life. And he was the man a national network threw away in 1971 as a relic, who answered it by building his own network out of 200 television stations and running for another 11 years in front of a bigger audience than they had given him.

None of that was ever on screen. What was on screen was bubbles.

There is a photograph in the mind of anybody who watched him. A beaming man in a dinner jacket, arms up, counting in a band. Think about what he was actually holding all those years, in a Los Angeles television studio, in front of 30 million people. It was the same instrument bought for $400 in 1920, paid for with four years of farm labor by a boy who had nothing else to trade, and carried off a homestead in Emmons County on the 11th of March, 1924, by a 21-year-old with $3 pinned inside his jacket who could not yet ask directions in English.

He never put it down. He just kept playing it until the whole country was listening.
