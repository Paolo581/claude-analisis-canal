Eugene Allen Hackman was born on January thirtieth, nineteen thirty, in San Bernardino, California. The family drifted east and ended up in Danville, Illinois, where his father ran the presses at the local newspaper.

When Gene was thirteen, his father left.

Hackman told the story many times, always the same way. He was out playing in the street. His father drove past in the car, and as he went by, he waved. Just a wave. And the boy understood, right there, that the man wasn't coming back.

He said later that it taught him how much a single small gesture can do to a person. He spent the rest of his life watching people's small gestures. That was the actor.

At sixteen, he lied about his age and joined the Marines. He served as a radio operator in China. When he came out, he bounced between jobs and cities until, at twenty-six, he enrolled at the Pasadena Playhouse to study acting.

There's a story from that school that every Hackman fan knows. He became friends with another student, a short, awkward kid named Dustin Hoffman. And according to the story, the two of them were singled out by their classmates as the ones least likely to succeed.

Both of them would win Academy Awards. Both of them would be nominated five times.

But first came New York, and years of nothing. Hackman worked as a doorman. He moved furniture. He drove a truck. On New Year's Day, nineteen fifty-six, he married Faye Maltese, a bank secretary, and they had three children. Christopher in nineteen sixty. Elizabeth in sixty-two. Leslie in sixty-six.

Keep those three names. They are going to walk back into this story at the very end, in a courtroom.

The break came in nineteen sixty-seven, with Bonnie and Clyde. Four years later he was Popeye Doyle in The French Connection, and in the spring of nineteen seventy-two he walked onto a stage in Los Angeles and picked up the Oscar for Best Actor.

He was forty-two. He had waited longer than almost anyone.

What followed was one of the great careers in American film. Lex Luthor in Superman. The coach in Hoosiers. The FBI man in Mississippi Burning. The sheriff in Unforgiven, which brought him his second Oscar in nineteen ninety-three. Close to a hundred movies.

And through all of it, one thing never changed. Gene Hackman could not stand being famous.

He didn't do the parties. He didn't do the talk shows if he could help it. He was known on sets as intense, impatient, and sometimes frightening. He said more than once that he was an actor, not a star, and that he wanted to be left alone.

In nineteen eighty-six, after thirty years, his marriage to Faye ended. And around that same time, at a gym in California, he met a young woman who was working there part-time to pay her bills.

Her name was Betsy Arakawa. She was a classical pianist. She was about thirty years younger than he was.

In nineteen ninety-one they got married. And Gene Hackman did what he'd been threatening to do for years. He left Hollywood, and he didn't go to Malibu or Montana like the other stars. He went to the high desert of New Mexico. Santa Fe.

What he built there is the first thing you need to understand about how he died.

The estate sat inside a gated community in the hills, on fifty-three acres. A main house with three bedrooms and a library where, according to the people who sold it, he spent his evenings watching movies. A guest house. A lap pool. A studio where he painted. A scattering of garages and sheds and outbuildings across the land.

It was not a fortress. There were no guards, no walls. It was something quieter than that. It was a place designed so that nobody would ever need to come by.

The Hackmans had no live-in staff. According to his daughters, they didn't even hire a housekeeper. Gene didn't carry a cell phone. He used the landline, and only the landline. When they needed something, Betsy went to town and got it.

And Santa Fe let them be. The real estate agents who handled the house in twenty twenty-six put it in one sentence. The Hackmans embraced Santa Fe, and Santa Fe embraced them. He bought his art supplies at the local store like everybody else. People saw him in restaurants and left him alone.

For a man who'd spent his life running from attention, it was paradise.

In two thousand four, he made his last movie, a comedy called Welcome to Mooseport. He never announced that he was retiring. He just stopped. Years later he said a doctor had run a stress test on his heart and told him that making movies was no longer something his body should be doing.

So he wrote. Three adventure novels with a friend named Daniel Lenihan, an underwater archaeologist, and then two more on his own. He painted. He rode his bicycle around Santa Fe, and in twenty twelve, at eighty-one, a car hit him while he was riding in the Florida Keys. He was flown to a hospital in Miami, and he walked out of it.

The man seemed indestructible. And the woman next to him made sure the world kept thinking so.

Betsy Arakawa was born in Honolulu, in December of nineteen fifty-nine. She was a child prodigy at the piano. At eleven years old she played a concert hall in Honolulu in front of nine thousand schoolchildren. She went to the University of Southern California, played with the Honolulu Symphony, and moved to Los Angeles to build a career in music.

Then she met Gene. And slowly, year by year, her life became his.

In Santa Fe, she opened a home furnishings store called Pandora's with her best friend, Barbara Lenihan, the wife of the man who co-wrote Gene's novels. She played the piano. She ran the house. Hantavirus is carried by deer mice. It lives in their droppings, their urine, their nests. You don't catch it from a bite. You catch it by breathing. You sweep out a shed, you open a garage that's been shut all winter, and the dust goes into the air.

For three to six days it feels like the flu. Fever, aches, a cough. Then, with no warning, the lungs begin to fill with fluid. The state's chief medical investigator, Doctor Heather Jarrell, said that once that stage begins, a person can be dead in twenty-four to forty-eight hours.

New Mexico sees only a handful of cases in a typical year. Most people who get it have never heard of it. Betsy's final searches, for COVID, for the flu, for dizziness and nosebleeds, tell you she was looking in the wrong place.
