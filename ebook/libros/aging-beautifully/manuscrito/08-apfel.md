## Chapter 8. Iris Apfel: A fashion model at ninety-seven

In 2019, one of the biggest modeling agencies in the world, the one that represents the supermodels you see on magazine covers, signed a new client.

She was ninety-seven years old.

Her name was Iris Apfel, and she had never been a model, an actress, or a singer. For most of her life she ran a fabric company with her husband Carl, restoring curtains and upholstery for old houses, including nine times for the White House. She was known, if at all, to interior decorators.

Then, in 2005, when she was eighty-four, the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York had a hole in its schedule. A show had fallen through. Somebody remembered that Iris Apfel had a closet, or rather a whole apartment, full of the most extraordinary clothes and jewelry anyone had ever seen. Couture jackets worn with flea-market beads. Fur with plastic bangles stacked to the elbow. Enormous round glasses. Colors that should not go together and somehow did.

The museum put her closet on display. The show was supposed to be a filler. It became one of the most popular exhibits the museum had ever held, and an eighty-four-year-old woman with white hair and giant black spectacles became, overnight, the most famous face in fashion.

She was on magazine covers at ninety. She had a documentary made about her at ninety-three. She designed a line for a department store at ninety-four. She signed the modeling contract at ninety-seven. She died in March 2024, at a hundred and two, with a closet still full of plans.

Her philosophy fit on a T-shirt, and in fact it was printed on one: **"More is more, and less is a bore."**

But under the beads, there was a sharper message, and it is the one that matters here. "I don't do pretty," she said. "Pretty is boring. When you are young, you are pretty because you are young. After that, you have to be *interesting*." And: "If you are not interested, you are not interesting." She read. She traveled into her nineties. She went to parties. She learned new things on purpose. She was, in her own words, "the world's oldest living teenager."

And she had a rule about mirrors. She did not look in them to find fault. She looked in them to decide what to put on next.

### What this means for you

This chapter has no diet and no exercise in it, and it may be one of the most useful chapters in the book.

**How you dress changes how you feel, and how you feel changes your health.** Psychologists call it "enclothed cognition": putting on clothes that you associate with confidence, competence, or joy actually changes your mood, your posture, and even your performance on tests. Older women who make the effort to dress every day, rather than staying in a housecoat, report less depression and more energy. It is not vanity. It is self-respect, and it works from the outside in.

**Color lifts mood.** There is real research on this. Bright, saturated colors raise alertness and happiness, and they also make you more visible to drivers, which matters after sixty-five, when most pedestrian deaths occur. Iris Apfel's scarlet coat was a safety device.

**"Interesting" protects your brain.** We will say more about this in Part Four, but people who stay curious, who learn new things and seek out new experiences, build what scientists call cognitive reserve, a kind of cushion against memory loss. Boredom, not age, is the enemy.

**The one thing to be careful about is your feet.** This is the only place where fashion and safety fight, and safety should win. Floppy slippers, backless shoes, high heels, and smooth-soled flats cause a large share of the falls in older women. A good shoe has a low heel, a closed back, a sole that grips, and room for your toes. It can also be red.

### The Iris Apfel wardrobe test

Open your closet. Ask yourself three questions:

1. **What have I not worn in a year?** It is taking up room that something joyful could have.
2. **What do I feel good in?** Put it where you can reach it. Wear it on an ordinary Tuesday.
3. **What is one thing I have always wanted to wear and never dared?** A red lipstick. A big necklace. A hat. Buy it. Wear it this week. Iris would.

### Three things to do this week

1. **Get dressed every day,** even if no one will see you. Shoes on. One piece of jewelry.
2. **Check your shoes.** Line them up. Any pair with a loose back, a worn sole, or a heel higher than an inch and a half goes to the back of the closet or to charity.
3. **Do one interesting thing.** A museum, a library talk, a new recipe, a bus to a part of town you have never visited. If you are not interested, you are not interesting.
