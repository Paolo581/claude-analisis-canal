## Chapter 9. Dolly, 2025: "I have to keep busy"

Carl Dean died on March 3, 2025, at eighty-two. He had been ill for some time; Dolly had cancelled a run of Las Vegas shows the year before, citing family matters, and had said little. When he died, she said less: a statement thanking people for their prayers, asking for privacy. The man who had never given an interview was buried the way he had lived, quietly.

Then, within days, she released a song she had written for him, called "If You Hadn't Been There." And she went back to work.

That is the part that people found hard to understand. A widow of three days, back in the studio. But when she finally spoke about it, a few weeks later, she said what every widow in America understood instantly.

"I have to keep busy. If I sat still, I don't know what I'd do."

And then, with the honesty that is the reason people love her: "I cry every day. I talk to him. The house is so quiet. But I've got to keep going, because that's what he'd want, and because I don't know any other way."

She was seventy-nine. In the months that followed, she kept every commitment she could. She postponed some, and was honest about why: she said in the fall of 2025 that she had some health problems of her own to deal with, that she was not dying, and that people should stop praying so hard. She kept the Imagination Library mailing books. She turned eighty in January 2026 and said she did not plan to retire, because retirement would mean sitting still, and she had already told the world what she thought of that.

What she has not done, as far as anyone can tell, is pretend. She has not said she is fine. She has said she is working.

### What this means for you: the twelve months

Dolly's instinct is right, with one addition that she would be the first to accept: keeping busy only works if it includes eating, sleeping, taking your medicine, and letting people in. Here is the first year, in order.

**The first month: your body.** The shock of a spouse's death can injure the heart. Doctors call it takotsubo cardiomyopathy, or broken heart syndrome: a surge of stress hormones stuns the heart muscle, and it feels exactly like a heart attack. It mostly happens to women over sixty, most often in the weeks after a loss, and it is usually recoverable if treated. Chest pain, pressure, or shortness of breath in the first weeks after a death is an emergency. Call 911. Do not assume it is grief.

In the same month: eat something with protein three times a day, even if you do not want it. Sleep when you can. Take your pills. Let someone sit with you.

**The first three months: do not decide.** Do not sell the house, move in with a child, give money away, lend money, or change your will unless you must. Grief clouds judgment, and the first weeks bring both people who want to help and people who want to take advantage. Anyone who pressures a new widow to decide anything quickly is to be refused.

**The first six months: the paperwork, slowly, with help.** Death certificates, the will, Social Security survivor benefits (call 1-800-772-1213; you may be entitled to his benefit if it is higher than yours), his pension, life insurance, the bank accounts, the house title, the car. Ask a child, a friend, or a lawyer to sit with you for this. Nobody should do it alone. The Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 can point you to free legal help for seniors.

**All year: the room.** Find a bereavement group. Hospices run them free, for anyone in the community, not only their own families. Churches run them. Senior centers run them. Once a week, in a room with other women who have lost husbands, is the single most effective thing a widow can do for her health in the first year. You do not need to talk. You need to be in the room.

**All year: keep busy, Dolly's way.** Work, if you have it. Volunteer, if you don't. The Imagination Library is Dolly's; yours may be the church kitchen, the library, the grandchildren, the shelter. Purpose is not a distraction from grief. It is the thing that carries you through it.

**After a year: notice.** Most widows describe the second year as different from the first: the grief does not end, but it stops being the whole sky. If that has not happened, if you are still not eating, not sleeping, not going out, see your doctor. Grief that does not move has become something treatable.

### Three things to do this week

1. **If you have lost someone recently, call a hospice and ask about bereavement groups open to the community.** Go once.
2. **Write down the three decisions people are pressing you to make,** and beside each one write "not before" and a date at least six months away.
3. **Keep busy at one thing that needs you tomorrow morning.** Dolly's is books. Choose yours.
