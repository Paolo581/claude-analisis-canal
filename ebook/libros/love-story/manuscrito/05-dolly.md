# Part Two: The Long Love

## Chapter 5. Dolly and Carl: Fifty-eight years

On her first day in Nashville, in 1964, eighteen-year-old Dolly Parton walked out of a laundromat and a man in a pickup truck drove by and waved. His name was Carl Dean. He was twenty-one, and he laid asphalt for a living. He has said, in one of the perhaps three sentences he ever gave to the press, that his first thought was that he was going to marry that girl.

They married two years later, on May 30, 1966, in a small church in Georgia, because her record label had told her not to get married and she did not want to argue with them in Tennessee. Her mother was the only guest.

For the next fifty-eight years, Carl Dean did not go to her concerts. He did not walk red carpets. He did not give interviews, appear in photographs, or, as far as anyone can tell, care at all that his wife had become one of the most famous people on earth. He ran his paving business, and then retired, and mowed the lawn, and fixed things, and waited for her to come home.

The tabloids spent decades insisting the marriage was a sham, that he did not exist, that she was secretly with her best friend, that they lived apart. Dolly laughed at all of it and said the same thing every time: "He's the only man I ever loved. He's a good man, and he loves me for me."

So what was the secret of fifty-eight years? She has been asked ten thousand times, and her answers, put together, make a short and practical list.

**They were apart a lot.** She has said, more than once and only half joking, that the secret to a long marriage is to "stay gone." She was on the road for months. He was at home. When she came back, they were glad to see each other. "You can't get sick of somebody you don't see."

**They had separate lives and one home.** He had his friends, his tractor, his quiet. She had her career, her band, her Dollywood. They did not try to share everything. They shared a house, a bed, a sense of humor, and a set of memories that went back to a laundromat.

**They laughed.** Every account of the two of them, from the few people who saw them together, describes the same thing: he teased her, she teased back, and they laughed. She has said that she never married a man who could not make her laugh, and that she only married one man.

**She never made him be someone else.** He did not want to be in her world, and she did not drag him into it. She has said that if she had made him come to the shows and the parties, the marriage would have been over in five years.

**They kept a little romance.** Picnics in the truck, by her account. Drives to nowhere. A birthday song written just for him. Small things, kept up for six decades.

Carl Dean died on March 3, 2025, at eighty-two. Dolly released a song for him within days. She was seventy-nine.

### What this means for you

The research on marriages that last fifty years looks a great deal like Dolly's list, and it has a few things to add.

**Long marriages are not conflict-free. They are repair-rich.** Couples who last argue about as much as couples who split. The difference is that they repair quickly: a joke, a touch, an apology, going to bed friends. Dolly's humor is a repair tool.

**Space is not distance.** Couples who keep separate friends, interests and even separate rooms for hobbies report more satisfaction, not less. This matters enormously in retirement, which the next chapter covers.

**Acceptance beats improvement.** Trying to change a spouse of forty years is the most reliable way to make both of you miserable. Dolly never tried to make Carl like the spotlight. The research calls it "accepting influence" and "positive sentiment override," which means, in plain words, deciding to see the good.

**The marriage needs its own small rituals.** A Friday supper. A Sunday drive. A song. Couples who keep one or two rituals through the decades describe feeling "still married," not just still together.

### Three things to do this week

1. **If you are married, go somewhere for an hour, alone, that is yours.** Then come home and see if he is glad to see you.
2. **Say the one thing you have been trying to change in him, out loud, and then let it go** for seven days. See what happens to the house.
3. **Revive one ritual** that you used to have and dropped: the Friday supper, the drive, the song. Just once this week.
