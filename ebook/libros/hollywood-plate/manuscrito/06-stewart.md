## Chapter 6. Martha Stewart: The green glass, the garden, and the swimsuit at eighty-one

Every morning at six, before she looks at a phone or a newspaper, Martha Stewart stands in her kitchen and drinks a tall glass of something green.

She has described it so many times that fans make it along with her. A big handful of spinach. A few stalks of celery. Half a cucumber. A piece of fresh ginger. Some parsley. A little fresh orange or apple to take the edge off. Through the juicer, into a glass, drunk standing up. Then she puts on her boots and goes outside.

She was eighty-one when *Sports Illustrated* put her on the cover of its swimsuit issue, and the first question everyone asked was what diet she had gone on. She had gone on none. "I didn't starve myself," she said. "I ate what I usually eat." What she usually eats, it turns out, is one of the most sensible plates in this book, because most of it comes out of her own dirt.

Martha Stewart has a working farm, and she works it. Her vegetables are her vegetables. Her eggs come from her chickens. She eats a great deal of fish and very little red meat. Lunch is often a big salad with an egg or some fish on it. Dinner is fish or chicken with whatever the garden gave her that day. She drinks a lot of water and a little wine. She is wary of sugar and almost never eats candy or soda, which is a striking thing for a woman who has built an empire on cakes.

About the cakes: she eats them. A slice, on the day she makes one. She has said, more than once, that moderation is not deprivation, and that the person who bans dessert ends up eating a box of cookies at midnight.

The green juice gets the headlines, but the garden is the secret. A woman who spends two hours a day planting, weeding, pruning and hauling is doing more exercise than most gym members, and she is eating what she grows, which means her plate is mostly vegetables without her having to think about it.

### What a doctor would say today

**Leafy greens may be the single best food for an aging brain.** A long study in Chicago that followed nearly a thousand older adults found that those who ate about one serving a day of greens, spinach, kale, collards, lettuce, had brains that worked as if they were eleven years younger than those who rarely ate them. One serving. A handful in your eggs, a salad at lunch, spinach stirred into soup.

**Juice is good; the whole vegetable is better.** A juicer throws away the fiber, which is what keeps you full, keeps your bowels regular, and keeps blood sugar steady. A blender keeps it. If you want Martha's morning glass, make it a smoothie, or simply eat the salad.

**Gardening is a prescription.** Older adults who garden have lower rates of heart disease, better hand strength, better bone density, less depression, and in some studies lower rates of dementia. It gets you into the sunlight that sets your sleep clock and makes vitamin D. A few pots on a porch will do.

**Sugar is the thing to be suspicious of, not fat.** Added sugar in sodas, sweetened coffee, cereal, flavored yogurt, and "healthy" granola bars raises blood sugar and feeds inflammation, and is tied to heart disease and memory loss. The daily sweet tea does more damage than Martha's monthly slice of cake.

### Martha's table

#### The morning green smoothie for one

Her juice, with the fiber kept in. Three minutes in a blender.

A big handful of spinach or kale. Half a cucumber, chopped. A stalk of celery. A small piece of fresh ginger, peeled. Half an apple or a small orange, peeled. A cup of water or unsweetened almond milk. Blend until smooth. For a breakfast that holds you until lunch, add half a cup of plain Greek yogurt or a scoop of protein powder; that turns 90 calories into 200 with 15 grams of protein.

#### The big lunch salad for one

What she eats most days. Fill a wide bowl with greens: romaine, spinach, arugula, whatever you have. Add a cup of chopped vegetables: cucumber, tomato, bell pepper, carrot, radish. Add your protein: two boiled eggs, or a can of tuna or salmon, or a cup of chickpeas, or leftover chicken. Add something crunchy: a tablespoon of sunflower seeds or walnuts. Dress with Julia's vinaigrette. A slice of whole-grain bread on the side. About 400 calories and 25 to 30 grams of protein depending on the protein.

#### The roasted vegetable tray

Make this on Sunday and eat from it all week. Heat the oven to 425°F. Cut into chunks any three or four of: sweet potato, carrots, beets, Brussels sprouts, cauliflower, broccoli, red onion, bell peppers, zucchini. Toss with two tablespoons of olive oil, salt, pepper, and a teaspoon of dried herbs. Spread on two sheet pans and roast thirty to forty minutes until browned at the edges. Keep in the refrigerator. Add to eggs, salads, soups, grain bowls, or eat cold with a spoon of hummus.

#### Baked eggs in a nest of greens for one

Her chicken-and-garden breakfast. Wilt two big handfuls of spinach or chard in a small ovenproof skillet with a teaspoon of olive oil and a clove of garlic. Make two hollows in the greens and crack an egg into each. Season. Bake at 375°F for ten to twelve minutes until the whites are set. Eat with toast. About 220 calories and 14 grams of protein.

#### Lemon fish in a packet for two

The simplest fish dinner in the world, no smell, no mess. Heat the oven to 400°F. Tear two squares of foil or parchment. On each, put a 5-ounce fillet of white fish, a few thin slices of lemon, a handful of thinly sliced zucchini or asparagus, a teaspoon of olive oil, salt, pepper and a few sprigs of dill or parsley. Fold the packet closed. Bake fifteen minutes. Open at the table. About 220 calories and 28 grams of protein.

#### A slice of cake, the Martha way

Bake it yourself, on a Sunday, when the grandchildren are coming. Eat one slice, with them, at the table. Send the rest home with them.
