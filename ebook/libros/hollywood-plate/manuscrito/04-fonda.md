## Chapter 4. Jane Fonda: Why she eats more protein at eighty-eight than she did at forty

In the 1980s, Jane Fonda sold twenty million exercise videos and ate almost nothing.

She has written about it with a frankness that still startles people. From her teens into her forties she was bulimic, bingeing and purging in secret, sometimes several times a day, while she filmed movies and won Oscars and told American women how to feel the burn. "I was not a healthy person," she wrote. "I was obsessed with being thin." The workout empire, she admitted, was built partly on an illness.

Then, in her late forties, she stopped. She got help. And over the next forty years she did something that very few women of her generation managed: she learned to eat for strength instead of for size.

Here is what that means at eighty-eight, in her own words from interviews and from her book on the "third act" of life, *Prime Time*. "I eat more protein now than I ever did." Fish, chicken, eggs, beans, Greek yogurt, at every meal. Because, she explains, she learned from her doctors that the muscle she needs to stay upright, to get out of a chair, to not break a hip, is made of protein, and after sixty the body needs more of it, not less, to hold on to that muscle.

She eats a great deal of vegetables, "half the plate." She eats fruit, nuts, and whole grains. She does not eat much sugar, and she says plainly that she feels the difference when she does. In her eighties she stopped drinking alcohol because, she said, it affects an older body far more than a younger one and she wanted her sleep back.

What she does not do is diet. "I've done that," she has said. "It nearly killed me." She eats three real meals. She eats breakfast, which she skipped for decades. She keeps protein bars in her purse for long days on set. And she lifts weights, slowly, a few times a week, so that the protein has somewhere to go.

Two cancer diagnoses, a hip replacement, a knee replacement, and eighty-eight years in, Jane Fonda is stronger than she was in the leotard. She would be the first to tell you that the leotard was never the point.

### What a doctor would say today

Jane Fonda's doctors told her something that most women over sixty-five are never told clearly, and it is the most important sentence in this book:

**After 65, you need more protein than you did at 40.** The standard advice for younger adults is about 0.36 grams of protein per pound of body weight. Researchers who study aging now recommend about 0.45 to 0.55 grams per pound for people over sixty-five, and more if you are ill or recovering from surgery. For a woman who weighs 150 pounds, that is roughly 70 to 80 grams a day. Most older American women eat about 50, and much of it at dinner.

**Spread it across the day.** Your body can only use about 25 to 30 grams at a time to build muscle. A breakfast of toast and coffee, a lunch of soup, and a dinner with a big piece of chicken wastes most of the chicken. Aim for 25 grams at each meal. In food, that is:

- 2 eggs plus a cup of Greek yogurt, or
- A 3-to-4-ounce piece of fish, chicken, or lean meat (the size of your palm), or
- A cup of cottage cheese, or
- A cup of beans or lentils plus a slice of cheese, or
- A cup of Greek yogurt with a handful of nuts.

**Muscle is what keeps you independent.** The protein only works if you ask the muscle to grow: a few minutes of strength work with soup cans and a chair, as in our first book, two or three times a week. Protein plus movement is the combination that study after study finds keeps women out of nursing homes.

**Sugar and alcohol hit an older body harder.** Blood sugar swings are larger, sleep is lighter, and both are tied to memory. Fonda's choice to cut them was not vanity.

### Jane's table

#### The Greek yogurt breakfast bowl for one

Her most common breakfast, and the easiest 25 grams of protein in this book. Two minutes.

A cup of plain Greek yogurt (2 percent or whole milk, which keeps you full longer). A handful of berries, fresh or frozen. A tablespoon of chopped walnuts or almonds. A spoon of ground flaxseed if you have it. A drizzle of honey only if you must. About 250 calories and 22 grams of protein. Add a boiled egg on the side for 28.

#### Salmon with greens for two

Her standard dinner. Fifteen minutes.

Heat the oven to 425°F. Put two salmon fillets (5 ounces each) on a foil-lined pan, skin down. Brush with a teaspoon of olive oil, season with salt, pepper, and a squeeze of lemon. Roast twelve minutes. Meanwhile, warm a tablespoon of olive oil in a skillet, add a clove of sliced garlic and a big bag of spinach or chopped kale, and cook until wilted, two or three minutes. Season. Serve the salmon on the greens with lemon. About 350 calories and 32 grams of protein. Canned salmon, drained and mashed with a little lemon and a spoon of yogurt, is the budget version and just as good for you.

#### Red lentil soup for four

Lentils are the cheapest protein in the supermarket and cook in twenty minutes. This is a Fonda lunch.

- 1 tablespoon olive oil
- 1 onion and 2 carrots, chopped
- 2 cloves garlic, chopped
- 1 teaspoon ground cumin, ½ teaspoon turmeric if you have it
- 1 cup red lentils, rinsed
- 1 can (14 ounces) diced tomatoes
- 4 cups low-sodium broth
- Juice of half a lemon, salt and pepper

Cook the onion and carrots in the oil until soft. Add the garlic and spices for one minute. Add the lentils, tomatoes and broth. Simmer twenty minutes until the lentils collapse. Blend part of it if you like it smooth. Add the lemon, taste for salt. About 230 calories and 14 grams of protein per bowl. Top with a spoon of Greek yogurt for 18.

#### Cottage cheese plate for one

Her on-set lunch. A cup of cottage cheese, sliced tomato and cucumber, black pepper, a few crackers or a slice of whole-grain bread, a piece of fruit. About 300 calories and 28 grams of protein. Cottage cheese is the most underused food in the American refrigerator for women over sixty-five.

#### Chicken and vegetable sheet pan for two, with leftovers

The dinner she makes when she wants to cook once and eat twice. Thirty-five minutes.

Heat the oven to 425°F. On a large sheet pan, toss two chicken breasts or four thighs with two cups of chopped vegetables (broccoli, bell pepper, red onion, zucchini, cherry tomatoes), two tablespoons of olive oil, salt, pepper, and a teaspoon of dried oregano or Italian seasoning. Spread out in one layer. Roast twenty-five to thirty minutes. Serve half tonight with a scoop of brown rice or a slice of bread. Chop the rest cold into tomorrow's salad. About 380 calories and 35 grams of protein.
